    The Seven Science Skills

    Lesson 1. Expect a "name the skill from the description" question — that was three of the five Jeopardy clues in this category. Learn them by their one-line definition, not the paragraph.

    Observation
    Using your five senses to notice both the big picture and the fine details. The most basic skill — it's what generates the questions you then investigate.
    Classification
    Grouping things by similarities so you can categorize the world. Can be simple (counting a bug's legs) or complex (naming exact species).
    Measurement
    Using tools (ruler, beaker, balance) accurately and precisely to collect numerical data.
    Prediction
    An educated guess about what will happen, based on previous observations or background knowledge. Requires critical thinking.
    Controlling variables
    Identifying, tracking, and holding variables constant so you can tell what actually caused a change in your results.
    Interpretation
    Two parts: (1) identifying trends in your results, and (2) working out what those trends mean. Recording an outcome is easy; interpreting it is the hard half.
    Communication
    Sharing findings clearly through words, models, and graphs — tables, written work, and presentations. It also helps you absorb and analyze the information yourself.
    Likely question

    Why does communication matter? It makes knowledge accessible to other scientists and to stakeholders, so it can influence policy, medicine, and societal progress. Three ways to communicate data: tables, graphs, and the written or spoken word (papers, presentations).

    Lab Safety & Participation

    Lesson 1, from the Safety Agreement you signed, the Fire Safety guidelines, and the Participation Rubric. Cluster these into four groups rather than memorizing fifteen separate rules.

    The four clusters

    Behavior
    Follow all verbal and written directions; ask if unclear. Always walk — no running, playing, or goofing around. Report every accident, injury, or broken item to the teacher immediately.
    Dress & PPE
    Goggles or glasses when indicated — no exceptions; apron and gloves when directed. Tie hair back, no dangling jewelry, no loose or baggy clothing. Improper attire = not admitted to the lab.
    Chemicals & equipment
    Treat every chemical as dangerous. Never touch, taste, or smell anything unless told to. Never mix anything undirected. No eating or drinking. Cut away from your body; never pick up hot or broken glassware. Nothing goes down the sink unless instructed.
    Workspace
    Keep the area clear and aisles free (backpacks at the front). Know where the exits and safety equipment are. At the end: clean, rinse, wipe dry, return equipment, dispose of waste properly, wash hands with soap and water.

    Fire safety

    Never leave an open flame unattended. Before any lab you should know the location of two exits from the building (not elevators), the nearest fire alarm pull stations, and the fire extinguishers in and near your lab.

    If you discover a fire: notify people in the area (yelling is appropriate here) → pull the alarm if needed → only attempt to extinguish if it is safe to do so → evacuate the immediate area → shut the lab doors and corridor doors behind you.

    Memorize: PASS

    Stand 6–8 feet from the fire with your back to an unobstructed exit, then:
    Pull the pin to unlock the operating lever.
    Aim low — at the base of the fire, not the flames.
    Squeeze the handle to discharge the agent (hold the hose tightly — there's pressure).
    Sweep side to side, and front to back.

    Never assume the fire is out. Watch for re-ignition and wait for the fire department.

    Data Tables & Graphs

    Lesson 2. The biggest section on the test: you'll have to build a table, pick the right graph, spot what's broken in a graph, and read trends out of one.

    Data table — the four required elements

    A data table is a method for organizing information (usually numerical) visually and understandably, normally from observation or experimentation. Every table you make needs all four:

    1. Title describing the variables. Use words to say what the data shows, and name the IV and DV. Standard format: "The Effect of ___ on ___." It may also mention controlled variables. Example: "The Effect of Heat on Cricket Chirp Rate."
    2. Headings with units. Each heading gives the variable's name and its unit of measurement for quantitative data — e.g. Air Pressure (lbs/cm²).
    3. Columns for the data, with trials in separate columns. The IV levels run down the left; each trial gets its own column; the average goes in the far-right column.
    4. Data to the correct precision, with the same number of decimal places throughout — including the average.

    Vocabulary you'll be asked for

    Raw data is what you actually measured. Derived data (also called processed data) is a value found mathematically rather than measured — an average is the classic example. It isn't "real" data; nobody observed it.

    Picking the right graph

    Change over time
    Line graph

    Use when you want to see how data changes over time. Multiple lines let you compare several data sets at once. A steep curve = a fast rate of change.

    Oxygen produced by a plant, measured hourly for 24 hours.
    Comparing categories
    Bar graph

    Use to compare different data sets or groups, especially when the specific values matter. Stacked bars can show the composition of broader categories.

    Salaries of four professions: teacher, vet, engineer, banker.
    Parts of a whole
    Pie chart

    Use for percentages / proportions of a whole; subgroups must total 100%. Best for relative proportions, not specific values. Falls apart past about five categories.

    Percentage of students in the class who are male vs. female.
    Relationship between two numbers
    Scatter plot

    Use to compare two numeric variables and visualize the relationship between many separate data points. Add a line of best fit to show the correlation.

    Hours spent studying vs. test scores; cricket leg length vs. antenna length.

    Building a graph correctly

    • Independent variable on the x-axis, dependent variable on the y-axis. This is the single most-tested rule in the section — it was one of the two errors in the Jeopardy "what's wrong with this graph" clue.
    • Both axes need units. "Height" is wrong; "Height (cm)" is right. That was the other error.
    • Axes drawn and scaled correctly, labeled clearly, data plotted accurately.
    • Legend present if needed; title and/or caption present; trend line shown on scatter plots and line graphs.
    • Neat, legible, and it actually helps answer the question.

    Before graphing anything, identify the IV, the DV, and the range of each. For the turtle-egg data (16–26 °C, 3–17 eggs hatched), the IV is temperature with range 16–26 and the DV is number hatched with range 3–17.

    Trends: correlation vs. relationship

    These are two different pairs of words and the test can ask for both about the same graph.

    Positive correlation
    The slope of the data runs up and to the right.
    Negative correlation
    The slope runs down and to the right. (Data can also have no correlation at all.)
    Direct relationship
    Both variables move the same way — both increase, or both decrease. Higher temperature → higher perspiration rate. Wider pipe → more water flow.
    Inverse relationship
    One variable increases while the other decreases. More exercise → lower heart-disease risk. The farther you drive, the less distance remains.
    Line of best fit
    The trend line drawn through scattered points. It lets you derive unknown data points and make predictions beyond the values you measured.

    Why data visualization is done well or badly

    From the Ella Worsdale blog reading: pie charts get a bad reputation because the human brain struggles to compare angles. With little-league runs in a pie chart you can just about pick out first place, but not second. Put the same data in a single-color bar chart sorted by size and the ranking is instantly readable. The point of data visualization is to make data accessible and enable understanding — if a chart isn't easy to interpret, it's simply the wrong choice.

    Measurement, Accuracy & Precision

    Lesson 3. This section is where points are won and lost on arithmetic, so do the conversions with a method rather than by instinct.

    Quantitative vs. qualitative

    Quantitative data is based on numbers — measurements taken with tools, or counting. Qualitative data is based on description and interpretation, and is not numerical.

    The metric system

    Base units: meter (m) for distance, gram (g) for mass, liter (L) for volume. The metric system works because the same prefix scale applies to all of them — a kilogram relates to a gram exactly the way a kilometer relates to a meter.

    Kilok—1000 units
    Hectoh—100 units
    Dekada—10 units
    Basem / g / L1 unit
    Decid—0.1 units
    Centic—0.01 units
    Millim—0.001 units
    ← climbing UP: ÷10 per step, decimal moves LEFTclimbing DOWN: ×10 per step, decimal moves RIGHT →

    You are expected to have these prefixes and their values memorized. Count the steps between the two prefixes, then move the decimal that many places in that direction. 812 cm → m is two steps up: 8.12 m. 1.7 kg → g is three steps down: 1700 g.

    Factor-label method (dimensional analysis)

    The safer method when the jump is large. Convert 240,500 cm to km:

    240,500 cm  ×  (1 m / 100 cm)  ×  (1 km / 1000 m)  =  2.405 km

    1. Write your starting unit.
    2. Convert to the base unit. Tip: list the larger unit as 1, and put how many of the smaller unit make 1 of the larger.
    3. Convert to the final desired unit the same way. The unwanted units cancel diagonally.

    Accuracy vs. precision

    Accuracy
    How close your data is to the correct (accepted) value. Think: hitting the bullseye.
    Precision
    How consistent your data is — how close the measurements are to each other. Think: a tight cluster, wherever it lands.

    On a target diagram: a tight cluster on the bullseye is both; a tight cluster off to one side is precise but not accurate; points scattered evenly around the bullseye average out correctly but are accurate-ish, not precise; points scattered off to one side are neither. Experimental data needs both to be valid and credible.

    Three limits on every measurement

    1. The accuracy of the measurer. 2. The accuracy of the observer (often the same person). 3. The precision of the tool. Because tools and observers are both imperfect, all measurements contain error — which is why 100 mL measured in a beaker does not read as exactly 100 mL when poured into a graduated cylinder.

    How to actually take a reading

    • Measure, then estimate one decimal place past what you can clearly see. If the smallest markings are whole mL, you report tenths of an mL. This is why an answer like 38.0 mL is correct and 38 mL is not — the zero is the estimated digit and it has to be there.
    • Liquids: always read the bottom of the meniscus, the bottom of the curve, at eye level. Liquids stick to the sides of the container and won't show a straight line.
    • A graduated cylinder is more precise than a beaker because its scale (its "graduations") is finer — more markings means more digits you can legitimately report.
    • Tools can be built at different scales, so read the numbers on the tool in front of you rather than assuming.
    Reporting derived data

    An average must be reported to the same precision as the raw data it came from. If your measurements had two decimal places and your calculator says 638.6794125 m, you report 638.68 m. Do not copy the whole calculator display — and do not round away real precision either.

    Introduction to Chemistry

    Lesson 4. Chemistry is the branch of science that deals with the identification and properties of different types of matter, especially when they interact and change to form new substances.

    Why a biologist learns it: living things are made of biological molecules. Chemistry explains the structure and function of those molecules, the processes living things carry out (metabolism, growth, reproduction), how enzymes speed up chemical reactions, and how DNA stores genetic information.

    Atoms and elements

    • All matter in the universe is made of atoms. An atom is the smallest unit of matter that retains its elemental properties. (From the Greek atomos — "uncuttable.")
    • An element is matter made of only one type of atom.
    • Individual atoms are mostly empty space.
    • The numbers of protons, neutrons, and electrons give elements their properties.

    The three subatomic particles

    "Sub-" means smaller, "-atomic" means atom — particles smaller than an atom. Be able to reproduce this table from memory.

    Subatomic particles
    ParticleLocationChargeMass (amu)
    ProtonNucleusPositive (+)1
    NeutronNucleusNeutral (none)1
    ElectronElectron cloudNegative (−)~0
    • The nucleus sits at the center of the atom and contains the protons and neutrons. The neutrons keep the nucleus stable.
    • The number of protons determines what element an atom is. This is a guaranteed question.
    • Electrons spin around the outside in the electron cloud, in distinct regions called energy levels. They gain or lose energy by moving between levels. Because they're on the outside, electrons are what interact with the world.

    Bonding

    A neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons. But atoms also "want" a full outermost energy level, so they give, take, and share electrons with other atoms. That giving, taking and sharing is chemical bonding, and it is the cause of chemical reactions. Which bond forms depends on how strongly the atoms pull on each other's electrons and how many electrons each needs.

    Ionic bond
    One atom takes an electron from another. Losing electrons leaves an atom positively charged; gaining them leaves it negatively charged. Any charged atom is an ion, and the ionic bond is the attraction between two oppositely-charged ions.
    Covalent bond
    Atoms share one or more electrons, so their charges don't change. Sharing more than one electron makes stronger double and triple bonds — nitrogen's triple bond is very hard to break.
    Molecule
    A group of two or more atoms held together by covalent bonds. Covalent bonding is the one that forms molecules — ionic bonding does not.

    Reading chemical formulas

    Two numbers, two jobs:

    • The coefficient is the big number in front — it counts molecules.
    • The subscript is the small number below — it counts atoms inside one molecule.
    Formula practice

    Reaction equations

    Reactants → Products. Reactants are what you start with (left of the arrow); products are what you end with (right of the arrow). In C6H12O6 + 6 O2 → 6 CO2 + 6 H2O, the products are CO2 and H2O.

    Charge calculation

    Charge = protons − electrons. An atom with 3 protons and 4 electrons has one extra negative charge, so its charge is −1. More electrons than protons → negative. Fewer → positive.

    Scientific Method & Experimental Design

    Lesson 5, and the section with the most written-response potential. You should be able to take any scenario and pull out the hypothesis, the three variable types, and both groups.

    The scientific method

    The general sequence: observation → question → hypothesis → experiment → data collection → analysis → communication. It's usually preceded by observation, and hypotheses guide the experiment and end up either supported or rejected.

    Guaranteed question: is it linear?

    No — false. The order of the steps can vary, you often need to repeat steps and loop back, and unexpected results can send you down an unexpected path. A wrong hypothesis isn't a failure; it's information that brings you closer to the right answer.

    The three kinds of variables

    Independent variable
    The variable you change on purpose. Goes on the x-axis.
    Dependent variable
    The variable you measure — it's affected by the independent variable. Goes on the y-axis.
    Controlled variables (constants)
    The variables kept constant so that only the IV can be causing changes in the DV. An experiment where everything except the IV is held constant is called a controlled experiment.

    Worked example. "How does the amount of water added affect how long it takes beans to sprout?" IV = amount of water. DV = length of time for beans to sprout. Controlled variables = amount of sunlight, amount and type of soil, temperature, bean variety, container size.

    Control group vs. experimental group

    • Control group: does not receive the treatment. It is the standard of comparison.
    • Experimental group(s): receive the treatment. You can have several at different levels.
    Worked scenarios from class
    ScenarioControl groupExperimental group(s)
    Does water amount affect bean sprouting?Beans with no water addedBeans with 10 mL, 20 mL, 30 mL…
    Do high-protein diets affect mouse weight?Mice on the standard dietMice on the high-protein diet
    Does salt concentration affect seed germination?Seeds soaked in water, no saltSeeds soaked in varying salt concentrations
    Does fertilizer enhance corn growth?Corn with no fertilizer addedCorn with fertilizer added

    Writing a hypothesis

    A hypothesis is a scientist's educated guess answering the question. It must:

    1. Include both the independent and dependent variables.
    2. Be testable.
    3. Be falsifiable — it has to be possible for the answer to come back "no."
    The classic trick question

    Which are required for a hypothesis? (A) written in "If…then…" format, (B) testable, (C) can be proven right, (D) can be proven wrong. — The answer is B and D. "If…then…" is a helpful format, not a requirement. And you can never prove a hypothesis right; you can only gather evidence that supports it. You can only prove one wrong.

    Why "People behave more immorally when there are fewer rules" fails: "immorally" isn't measurable and "fewer rules" isn't defined, so nothing here is testable. Fix it by naming a measurable DV and a specific IV — e.g. "If a posted rule against taking extra candy is removed, then the average number of candies each person takes will increase."

    Model hypotheses, in the format that shows the IV and DV clearly:

    • Plants kept at different temperatures for 2 weeks: "If bean plants are grown at higher temperatures, then their height gain over two weeks will increase."
    • Asthma drug vs. placebo: "If asthma patients take the drug daily for one month, then their clinical assessment scores will improve more than those of patients taking the placebo."

    1 · Cells, and cell theory

    The slides show four whole single-celled organisms (amoeba, euglena, bacterium, paramecium) next to cells taken from multicellular organisms (cardiac, cheek, leaf and onion cells). Cell theory has exactly three parts:

    1. Living things are made up of one or more cells — unicellular or multicellular.
    2. All cells come from pre-existing cells.
    3. The cell is the basic unit of structure and function in living things.

    Species Interactions

    When organisms live in communities, they interact constantly and have a powerful effect on the ecosystem. The slides name three types: competition, predation, and symbiosis (mutualism, commensalism, parasitism).

    Competition

    Competition occurs when organisms of the same or different species attempt to use the same ecological resource in the same place at the same time: food, water, shelter, space. A resource is anything required for life, such as food, water, light, nesting sites, or room to grow. When organisms compete for the same resource there is often a winner and a loser: the winner thrives and the loser fails to thrive and/or does not survive.

    Predation

    Predation is an interaction in which one organism captures and feeds on another. The predator does the killing and eating; the prey gets killed and eaten. Predation is a powerful force: it determines relationships in food webs and is a very effective regulator of population size.

    Predator adaptations
    Tools that make better predators

    Through natural selection, predators developed tools: snakes' heat-sensitive pits to locate prey; acute senses, fangs, claws, poison, stingers and sharp teeth; spiders' webs.

    Prey adaptations
    Avoid being captured

    (a) Run very fast to escape. (b) Camouflage to avoid detection, like the Vietnamese mossy frog. (c) Poisons advertised by bright warning colors.

    Plant adaptations
    Defenses against herbivores

    Sharp spines, thorns, sticky hairs and tough leaves, plus chemical compounds that are poisonous or bad-tasting. Also developed through natural selection.

    Mimicry

    Mimicry is a form of deception used by the prey as a means of defense against being eaten: a harmless species resembles a poisonous or distasteful species.

    King snake → mimic of the very poisonous coral snake. Viceroy butterfly → mimics the monarch. Birds avoid monarchs because of their very bitter taste. The viceroy doesn't taste bad, but since it resembles the monarch, birds are reluctant to eat it.

    Symbiosis

    Symbiosis is a close and permanent relationship between organisms of different species.

    Population Ecology

    Lessons 11–12 · Big QuestionHow do populations in an environment grow and change?

    A population is a group of individuals that belong to the same species that live in the same area at the same time. Its six important characteristics: size, geographic distribution, density, dispersion, growth rate, age structure.

    Population size
    The number of individuals in the population. A very important property, but it can be very difficult to measure. Daisies in a field are easy: the plants are immobile and confined to a small area, so you simply count them. More often individuals are too widespread, too abundant, or too mobile to count, like bullfrogs in Everglades National Park (1.5 million acres, 6,110 km²). Then scientists use methods of estimation, which have the potential for error.
    Population density
    The number of individuals per unit area. It varies tremendously depending on the species and the ecosystem. High: zebra mussels in the Hudson River, thousands per square meter. Low: grizzly bears in the Rocky Mountains, just 10–20 per 1000 km².
    Geographic distribution
    The range of the population: the area it inhabits. It can be just a few centimeters (mold on bread) or huge (the migration area of whales; the slide maps the blue whale's range across the world's oceans).
    Dispersion
    The spatial distribution of individuals within the population. Three types: clumped, uniform (even), random.

    The three types of dispersion

    Clumped

    Individuals are clustered together. Often happens when resources such as food, water or living spaces are clumped, or because of social behavior: herds, flocks of birds, schools of fish, hives of bees.

    Mussels and sea stars packed onto a rock.
    Uniform (even)

    Individuals are separated by a fairly even distance, usually the result of social interactions: individuals trying to get as far away from each other as possible.

    Penguins spaced apart by aggressive interactions between neighbors.
    Random

    The location of one individual is independent of the location of the others. May result from seed dispersal by the wind or animals.

    Dandelions scattered across a field.

    Population dynamics

    All populations are dynamic: they change in size and composition over time.

    Natality
    Increases population size through reproduction (births).
    Immigration
    Increases population size from external populations: the movement of individuals into an area.
    Mortality
    Decreases population size as a result of death.
    Emigration
    Decreases population size due to loss to external populations: the movement of individuals out of an area.
    Migration
    The movement of individuals into and out of a population (the wildebeest migration video: two million moving across the Serengeti).
    Life expectancy
    The length of time an individual is expected to live.
    Growth rate
    The amount by which a population's size changes in a given time.

    Population size = (Immigration + Natality) − (Mortality + Emigration)

    Births and arrivals push the size up; deaths and departures pull it down. To understand the changes in a population, all of these, along with life expectancy, must be considered. A population may remain very stable over time, or there can be significant fluctuations in size.

    Worked example

    30 births, 10 arrivals, 25 deaths, 5 departures: (10 + 30) − (25 + 5) = 40 − 30 = +10. The population grows by 10.

    07

    Population Growth

    What the "Oh Deer" activity showed: as resources become less available, growth slows or stops; as resources become more available, growth increases. The S-shaped curve of that pattern is called logistic growth.

    Exponential growth: unlimited resources and space

    • Under ideal conditions with unlimited resources, a population grows exponentially. Individuals reproduce at a constant rate.
    • Resources are unlimited, so there is no competition to limit the growth rate. It can be seen in populations that are very small or in regions newly colonised by a species.
    • At first growth is slow, because there is a shortage of reproducing individuals that may be widely dispersed. As the population increases, the number of reproducing individuals increases, so the growth rate increases: a J-shaped curve. The larger the population gets, the faster it grows.
    • The maximal growth rate for a given population is its biotic potential. The slide's exponential graph is labeled "growth without limits"; its elephant population graph (1900–1980) shoots up in the same J shape.
    One bacterium dividing into two every 20 minutes
    TimeStart20 min40 min1 hour2 hours1 day
    Cells1248644.72 × 1021

    That last number is 4,720,000,000,000,000,000,000 cells. "This growth doesn't seem too impressive at first." The 2-hour value just continues the slide's doubling (six doublings).

    Logistic growth and carrying capacity

    • Logistic growth occurs when a population's growth slows or stops following a period of exponential growth. When? When resources are depleted. That's why populations stop growing eventually.
    • Step 1: while the population is small, birth rates are high and death rates are low, so it grows nearly exponentially.
    • Step 2: when necessary environmental resources become limited, the birth rate becomes equal to the death rate, and growth stops.
    • The level where growth slows and stops is the carrying capacity (K): the average number of individuals a given environment can support, the region of the graph where growth levels off. Carry = to hold or contain; capacity = fullness, limit. The slide's logistic graph is labeled "competition limits growth".
    • Population size is limited by environmental factors like adequate food, shelter, water and mates. The slide's graph shows low food supply, disease, predators, limited space and nutrient availability pressing down on the curve at K.

    Real populations: overshoot, dieback and seasons

    • In reality, populations "overshoot" the environmental carrying capacity and then "dieback" below it, so the size fluctuates above and below the carrying capacity.
    • The seasonal graph: a breeding stock survives winter, a seasonal surplus builds in spring and summer, and the surplus is cut back by starvation, disease and parasites, accidents, weather, hunting, predators and other causes, leaving survivors at the carrying capacity by autumn.
    Assumption of both models

    The exponential and logistic models both assume the carrying capacity is constant and does not fluctuate. In reality, it changes with the environment. During a drought in a prairie there is less vegetation, so fewer prairie dogs survive and the carrying capacity is lower than normal.

    The models are not completely accurate representations of real populations, but they are useful tools scientists use to study populations.

    Biomass
    The 10% that passes to the next level is stored as biomass: energy built into an organism's body, to produce skin, muscle, nails and fat, and to grow (cell growth and division).
    Law of conservation of energy
    Matter/energy is not created or destroyed. The total amount of energy in a system remains the same (is conserved).
    The missing 90%
    It is used (homeostasis and metabolism) or lost as heat. Also, not all of the biomass gets eaten or absorbed (bones, waste, etc.).

    The "Photosynthesis and Cellular Respiration" diagram shows the cycle behind this: sunlight enters a plant's chloroplast (photosynthesis), making glucose and oxygen; an animal cell's mitochondria use them (cellular respiration), releasing carbon dioxide and water, ATP, and heat.

    Turn & Talk: eagles, rats, grasshoppers

    Predict: greatest to least population size is grasshoppers > rats > eagles.

    Explain: grasshoppers are primary consumers, rats are secondary consumers and eagles are quaternary consumers. Only about 10% of the energy stored as biomass passes up each trophic level, and the rest is used for metabolism or lost as heat, so each higher level has less energy and can support less biomass and fewer individuals. That is the answer to the Big Question.

    05Use the chocolate and insulin example to explain response to stimuli and homeostasis.

    Eating chocolate is a stimulus that raises blood glucose (a change in the internal environment). The insulin response lowers it again, bringing the body back to homeostasis, a state of internal balance.

    06Explain natural selection using the words mutation, adaptation and fitness.

    Random mutations in DNA make individuals differ in genetically determined traits. Traits that help an individual survive and reproduce better than others are adaptations, and individuals with them have higher fitness.

    Charles Darwin called this mechanism for evolution natural selection.

    07Define biotic and abiotic factors and give the slides' examples for a bullfrog.

    Biotic = living components of the environment; abiotic = nonliving factors that influence the ecosystem.

    Bullfrog biotic: availability of food, and other species that compete with it for food or space. Bullfrog abiotic: water availability and temperature.

    08Put the six levels of ecological organization in order, and explain how a community differs from an ecosystem.

    Biosphere → biome → ecosystem → community → population → organism.

    A community is all the living organisms in an area. An ecosystem is those organisms together with their nonliving, physical environment.

    09What is the difference between species richness and species evenness?

    Richness is the number of different species. Evenness (abundance) is the amount of individuals in each species, relative to each other.

    10What does a climograph show, and why is climate the best way to identify a biome?

    A climograph shows average monthly temperature (°C) and precipitation (mm) over a year.

    Climate, the long-term average temperature and precipitation based on 30 or more years of data, is the best indicator of biome type. Temperature and precipitation are the two abiotic factors with the greatest impact on where organisms live.

    11Name the biome: (a) 31 mm, 21.9 °C; (b) precipitation off the scale, 2,514 mm; (c) 575 mm, 3.6 °C; (d) wet winters, dry summers, 462 mm.

    Detritivores eat the remains of dead plants and animals (crab, vulture, snail, earthworm). Decomposers decay materials into smaller molecules that they eat and cycle back into the ecosystem (mushrooms, bacteria).

    14In the slides' food web, name the producer and the apex predator, what frogs eat, and what eats frogs.

    1 Producers (autotrophs). 2 Primary consumers (herbivores) eat producers. 3 Secondary consumers (carnivores or omnivores) eat primary consumers. 4 Tertiary consumers (usually top carnivore) eat secondary consumers.

    Quaternary consumers eat tertiary consumers.

    16What is a keystone species? Use the sea otter to explain.

    A species so important that if it were removed, all or part of the system would fail. It has a disproportionately large effect on its environment.

    Sea otters eat sea urchins and keep them under control, so kelp forests thrive. When otters decline, urchins surge and graze down the kelp.

    17Compare mutualism, commensalism and parasitism, with one slide example each.

    Mutualism: both benefit (birds eating ticks off hippos; termites and gut bacteria; ants and acacias). Commensalism: one benefits, the other is neither harmed nor helped (egrets and cows; barnacles on whales). Parasitism: one is helped, the other harmed (ticks, tapeworms).

    18Explain mimicry using both of the slides' examples.

    A harmless species resembles a poisonous or distasteful species, as a defense against being eaten.

    The king snake mimics the very poisonous coral snake. The viceroy butterfly mimics the monarch: birds avoid monarchs because they taste very bitter, so they avoid the look-alike viceroy too, even though it doesn't taste bad.

    19Ectoparasite vs. endoparasite, with examples.

    Ectoparasites live on the host but do not enter its body: leeches, ticks, fleas, lice. Endoparasites live inside the host's body: some bacteria, some protists, intestinal worms such as tapeworms.

    20Describe the three types of dispersion and what causes each.

    Clumped: clustered together, when resources are clumped or because of social behavior (herds, flocks, schools, hives). Uniform: fairly even spacing, from social interactions such as aggression between neighbors (penguins). Random: each location independent of the others, often from seed dispersal by wind or animals.

    21A population has 12 births, 3 immigrants, 20 deaths and 5 emigrants. Use the slide's equation.

    (Immigration + Natality) − (Mortality + Emigration) = (3 + 12) − (20 + 5) = 15 − 25 = −10. The population decreases by 10.

    22Compare exponential and logistic growth. What is carrying capacity?

    Exponential: unlimited resources, constant reproduction rate, J-shaped curve, growing faster as it gets larger. Logistic: growth slows or stops after a period of exponential growth when resources are depleted, giving an S-shaped curve.

    Carrying capacity is the average number of individuals an environment can support, where the curve levels off and the birth rate equals the death rate.

    23What are overshoot and dieback, and what assumption do both growth models make?

    Real populations overshoot the carrying capacity and then die back below it, fluctuating above and below.

    Both models assume carrying capacity is constant. In reality it changes: a prairie drought means less vegetation, fewer prairie dogs survive, and carrying capacity is lower than normal.

    24Explain top-down and bottom-up control with the slides' fox, frog, snail and plant graphics.

    Top-down: pressure from a higher trophic level. No foxes → more frogs → less snails → more plants (an oscillating cascade).

    Bottom-up: limited resources at lower levels. No fertiliser → less snails → less frogs → less foxes.

    25Sort: food shortage, tornado, toxic spill, disease spreading through a crowded colony, extra predators drawn to rising prey.

    Density-dependent: food shortage, disease spreading through a crowded colony, extra predators drawn to rising prey. Density-independent: tornado, toxic spill.

    26Describe the pattern on the wolf–moose graph and explain it.

    An increase in prey (moose) is followed by an increase in predators (wolves). More wolves kill more moose, so the moose population drops; then the wolves have less food and decline. This relationship controls the size of each population.

    27Producers store 1,000,000 kcal. How much reaches the primary, secondary and tertiary consumers?

    100,000 kcal, 10,000 kcal and 1,000 kcal: about 10% passes up at each step.

    28Where does the 90% of energy that isn't passed on go, and why isn't that a violation of the law of conservation of energy?

    It is used (homeostasis and metabolism) or lost as heat, and some biomass, like bones and waste, is never eaten or absorbed.

    The energy isn’t destroyed: it is used by the organisms or lost as heat, so the total amount of energy in the system stays the same.

    29Answer the Lesson 13 Big Question using eagles, rats and grasshoppers.

    As energy moves to higher trophic levels, only about 10% is passed on and stored as biomass; the rest is used or lost as heat.

    So the populations shrink going up: grasshoppers (primary consumers) > rats (secondary) > eagles (quaternary).

    From Foraging to Farming, and Life in Villages

    Article: Life in Ancient Villages / Village Networks. This is the origin story for everything else in the unit. If you can explain why farming created inequality, you can answer half the short-answer questions on the test.

    The advent of farming

    The switch from looking for food to growing it is called the advent of agriculture, and it did not happen everywhere at the same time or to the same degree. It happened gradually, over thousands of years, usually along river valleys and often pushed along by climate change — as regions dried out, resources shrank and people were driven toward rivers.

    The critical consequence: farming let people store extra food. Stored surplus meant not everybody had to spend all day getting food, which freed time for everything else — crafts, building, administration, religion, war.

    The chain to memorize

    Farming → food surplus → fewer farmers needed → job specialization (artisans, miners, metalworkers, scribes, soldiers, merchants) → population growth → villages become cities → demand for goods you cannot make locally → trade networks → wealth → social hierarchy → rulers who must protect and justify it all.

    Almost every "why did X happen" question in this unit is answered by naming the right link in this chain. Practice saying it out loud in one breath.

    Foraging life vs. early farming village life

    Hunter-gatherer communities compared with early farming villages
    FeatureForaging / hunter-gathererEarly farming village
    MovementMobile, following foodPermanent dwellings, settled
    Food securityNo storage; what you find is what you eatStorage and surplus, but dependent on weather
    EqualityLargely egalitarian — comparatively little inequalityStill fairly equal at first; inequality grows over time

    How historians know village life was roughly equal — then wasn't

    There are almost no written records from villages, so this is archaeology, not documents. The evidence:

    • Çatalhüyük (c. 7500–5700 BCE, modern Turkey) and the Tripolye culture (c. 5200–3500 BCE, modern Ukraine): homes were mostly the same size, and objects in homes and graves were of roughly equal value.
    • Analysis of human bones shows villagers ate similar diets — nobody was eating dramatically better.
    • Then burial sites start to look different from one another. Some graves — including children's graves — contain gold artifacts and jewelry while others contain nothing. Wealth in a child's grave is powerful evidence, because a child did not earn it: it means private wealth was being accumulated and passed down across generations. That is the birth of social class.

    Women in early villages

    • Labor was mostly divided by gender — women spent more time caring for small children while also producing food — but a division of labor is not automatically an inequality. Early on, it largely wasn't.
    • Women were often associated with fertility and the source of life. The Seated Mother Goddess of Çatalhüyük (c. 5500 BCE) is a clay goddess figure of exactly this type.
    • But celebrating the feminine did not produce gender equality. As permanent dwellings, ownership and stricter definitions of family arrived, gender hierarchies intensified. Surplus food allowed women to have more children, so women's lives shifted from food production toward children and the household, and male heads of family tended to gain control of wealth. Strategic marriage alliances made family lines even more rigid.

    Trade before long-distance trade

    Long before ships crossed oceans, villages traded with each other in local networks — with nomadic foragers and with other farming villages. Examples you should be able to name:

    • Çatalhüyük and obsidian. Obsidian is a hard volcanic glass used for tools and even mirrors. It was the specialty good of Çatalhüyük.
    • The Lapita culture (c. 1600–500 BCE) in Oceania: obsidian, ceramics, marine shells and plants moved between islands, proving inter-island trade happened well before long-distance routes existed.
    • Norte Chico (modern Peru), last millennium BCE: coastal villages traded fish, mollusks and shells inland for corn, textiles, potatoes, quinoa and llamas.

    Trade then feeds back on itself: villages that sat near trade hubs or controlled strategic crossings grew faster, which pulled more trade toward them. Coordinating roads, bridges, irrigation and drainage required administrators and leaders — another push toward hierarchy.

    Common mistake

    Do not say farming "made life better." The article is careful here: villagers lived in closer quarters so disease spread faster, they worried more about weather because their food supply depended on it, and health often got worse. Farming made societies bigger and more complex, not individually healthier. Aksum is the clean example — some East African communities were reluctant to shift to agriculture precisely because it meant a less healthy, more work-filled life.

    Archaeology: how we know any of this

    Archaeology
    The study of human history through excavation of sites and analysis of artifacts and other physical remains.
    Anthropology
    The scientific study of human culture — beliefs, customs, and the archaeological record.
    Artifact
    Any object made by human beings. Pottery, tools, seals, sculpture, coins.
    Excavation
    A dig carried out to search for and recover ancient artifacts and structures.
    Survivor bias
    What survives shapes what we know. Clothing and mud homes rot; pottery, shell, stone and metal last. So our picture of the past is skewed toward durable materials — and toward the rich, whose goods were made of durable materials.

    This is why societies without writing are not "less advanced" — they are less documented. Nok, Olmec, Chavín and the Indus Valley all left no readable texts, so everything we claim about them is an inference from objects. Expect a question that asks how the type of evidence available affects what we can know.

    Regional Trade Networks

    Article: Regional Trade Networks. The core comparison here — Afro-Eurasia vs. Mesoamerica — is the most likely single comparison on the whole test, because it is the one the slides return to over and over.

    Why trade existed at all: cities created demand

    The slide sequence is four boxes, and it is worth memorizing in that shape:

    Step 1
    Surplus

    New agricultural practices produce more food than the farmers need. Fewer people have to farm.

    Step 2
    Specialists

    Freed labor becomes artisans, miners, metalworkers — people who make things instead of food.

    Step 3
    Demand

    Cities need what they cannot grow or make, and wealthy elites want luxuries to display status.

    Step 4
    Trade

    Merchants, routes and markets appear to satisfy that demand. Cities are producers, consumers, and wealth.

    Because long-distance trade was slow, difficult and expensive, merchants concentrated on goods that were valuable, profitable and easy to carry: jade, silk, spices, porcelain, feathers, paper, textiles, dried fruit, nuts. That is why distant, very different societies ended up connected — only high-value goods justified the journey.

    Goods also got more expensive the farther they traveled, because merchants typically worked one leg of the route back and forth, handing goods to the next merchant — a relay race — and each merchant needed a profit.

    Afro-Eurasia vs. Mesoamerica — the essential contrast

    The one-sentence answer

    Why was the Afro-Eurasian network larger? Because it combined a head start (the world's oldest farming societies) with a geography that could actually be crossed — plains, navigable rivers, passable mountains — plus domesticated pack animals and sailing technology on predictable seas. Mesoamerica had none of those four advantages: jungle and mountains blocked movement, there were no pack animals, rivers were poor for long hauls, and canoes could not do what sailing ships did.

    If a question asks "geography or innovation — which mattered more?", the strongest answer says they compound: geography set the ceiling, and technology (camel saddles, sailing, road-building) determined how close a society got to it.

    Two pieces of evidence you should be able to name

    The Standard of Ur
    c. 2500–2400 BCE
    A Mesopotamian object from the city of Ur made almost entirely from materials not found in Mesopotamia: blue lapis lazuli from Bactria (Afghanistan), white shells from the Persian Gulf, and red marble from India. It is the classic proof that long-distance trade networks existed over 4,400 years ago — an artifact whose materials are the evidence.
    The Periplus of the Erythraean Sea
    1st century CE
    A sailing guide written in Greek that lists Indian Ocean ports, what was imported and exported at each, and what time of year to sail. It is written evidence that networks stretched from the Mediterranean to the Pacific — and it shows merchants planned around monsoon seasons.

    Mesoamerica in detail

    • Teotihuacan became the most important trade center in Mesoamerica by 300 CE, with over 100,000 inhabitants — the largest city in the Americas and one of the largest in the world.
    • Its power rested on controlling two important obsidian mines. Mesoamerican societies did not use metal for tools and weapons; they used obsidian. Whoever controlled obsidian controlled the region.
    • Jade was used in religious ritual, so cities that distributed jade gained religious as well as economic power.
    • Maya merchants moved cacao, jade, salt and quetzal feathers along the Yucatan coast; Zapotec merchants on the Pacific coast were known for cotton cloth and pottery.
    • The network moved ideas too — many Mesoamerican societies shared religious and cultural symbols spread along these routes.

    Why rulers cared about trade

    This is a government answer as much as a trade answer, and it is worth its own sentence on the test:

    • Trade brought in the wealth and resources needed to maintain a complex society — cities and empires are expensive.
    • So rulers built roads and bridges and used their armies to protect routes from bandits.
    • Some states and merchant communities developed common systems of weights, measures and coinage to make exchange efficient. (Sargon standardized weights and measures; Aksum minted its own coins.)
    • Extensive exchange changed social hierarchies and community structure in the societies it touched — new merchant classes, new elites, new displays of status.

    Ancient writers were just as split. A Mesopotamian poet praised Uruk's walls "gleaming like copper in the sun." City life was described as civilized, wealthy and educated — but also immoral and dangerous. Rural life was described as simple, pure and unspoiled — but uncivilized. That rural–urban divide is still argued about today, which is a good line to use in a short answer.

    Hierarchy: how cities organized people

    Hierarchy means ranking people into social levels. In cities, "civilized" came to mean city-dweller, and rural people were considered lower. Two groups held the structure in place:

    • Priests justified the king's rule — the religious argument for why the person on top belonged there.
    • Soldiers enforced authority — the physical argument.

    Keep those two together: legitimacy plus force is the pattern repeated in the Authority & Control section.

    Division of labor and job specialization

    • Farming surpluses are the basis — they allow non-farmers to exist at all.
    • New occupations: scribes, soldiers, kings, artisans, merchants.
    • Interdependence: a shoemaker in a city depends on farmers for food, soldiers for safety, and merchants for leather. Nobody is self-sufficient.
    • Villages are more self-sufficient; cities are highly interconnected. That is the sharpest one-line contrast between the two.
    • It even changed where people lived: in villages you live with your family; in cities you live near your job. Residence shifted from kinship to occupation.
    Likely short answer

    "Cities allowed job specialization, but city people still depended on rural farms. Why?" Because cities did not produce their own food. Specialization is only possible when someone else grows the surplus — so the more specialized a city became, the more dependent it was on the countryside, not less. This is also why conquest expanded agricultural production: taking territory meant more land, resources and labor to feed the city.

    Technology cities were forced to invent

    Large populations create problems that villages never had: sanitation, water supply, food supply, defense. The results: architecture, sewers, canals, irrigation, aqueducts, and underground water tunnels. (The Indus Valley is the standout example — see section 12.)

    The "urban archipelago"

    An archipelago is a group of islands. The author calls the ancient city network an urban archipelago because cities were islands of dense population and wealth in a sea of countryside, connected to each other by trade routes rather than by the land between them — a city often had more in common, and more contact, with a distant city than with the farms just outside its walls.

    Where the metaphor breaks down: islands are genuinely separate, but cities were not separate from their countryside — they were completely dependent on it for food, labor and raw materials. The land between cities was not empty water. Being able to state both halves of this is what earns full credit.

    Authority & Control in Ancient Empires

    Article: Authority & Control. The article's whole argument compresses into three tools. If you remember nothing else from this section, remember the three and one example of each.

    Tool 1
    Dynastic families

    Rule passed down a bloodline — or the convincing appearance of a bloodline. People must accept the family's right to rule.

    Han emperors traced descent from Gaozu; Roman emperors were adopted into dynasties to look legitimate.
    Tool 2
    Religion

    Divine legitimacy, enforced worship, or a unifying belief system.

    Mandate of Heaven; Cyrus the Great claiming Marduk chose him; Ashoka spreading Buddhism.
    Tool 3
    Force and violence

    Armies, punishment, destruction — used when toleration failed.

    The Jewish Revolt crushed, Jerusalem destroyed, Masada taken.

    Displays of power: Ashoka's pillars

    In the 3rd century BCE, the emperor Ashoka erected inscribed stone pillars across his empire proclaiming peace and Buddhist guidance. The crucial detail: most people could not read them. That is the point of the example — the monument itself communicated absolute power, regardless of the text. Empires use visible displays of power to maintain control.

    Dynastic legitimacy and the problem of women rulers

    • The typical pattern was male rulers passing power through the male line, and empresses were often limited to producing heirs.
    • Empress Theodora (Eastern Rome) — adviser to the emperor with significant real influence.
    • Empress Lü (Han China) — ruled as regent after Gaozu's death.
    • Hatshepsut (Egypt) — took the full title of king and ruled as a male-presenting pharaoh. Her coronation obelisk showed the gods watching over her coronation, specifically to confirm her status as a woman pharaoh.
    • The resistance is the evidence. Later rulers tried to erase Hatshepsut's inscriptions, and ancient graffiti at her tomb shows disapproval of female rule. When a question asks what challenges women faced as rulers, this is your example: they had to manufacture legitimacy that male rulers inherited, and it was attacked during and after their reigns.

    Religion as a control mechanism

    Divine right to rule
    The claim that the god or gods chose this ruler. It converts obedience into piety — disobeying the king becomes disobeying heaven.
    Mandate of Heaven
    tian ming, Zhou China
    The gods choose the ruler — but the mandate can be lost. A ruler who becomes corrupt or stops serving the people forfeits the right to rule, which justifies rebellion. This is the unusual one: most divine-right claims protect the ruler; this one contains a built-in excuse to overthrow him.
    Cyrus the Great (Persia)
    Claimed that Marduk, Babylon's own god, had chosen him — legitimizing his rule to the conquered rather than to his own people.
    Hittites (Anatolia)
    Seized the idols of conquered lands to demonstrate dominance over their gods.
    Neo-Assyrians
    Forced worship of their god Assur on conquered peoples.
    Ashoka
    Spread Buddhism to unify a diverse empire — religion as glue rather than as a weapon.
    Roman Empire
    Required public sacrifices to the gods as a loyalty test. Refusal meant punishment — which fell especially hard on Christians.

    Controlling the periphery (the edges)

    Why bother with the edges? Three reasons: collect taxes, prevent revolts, and recruit soldiers.

    Two methods: allow local rulers to stay in place (early Rome let conquered kings keep their thrones; Aksum's "king of kings" took tribute from smaller kings but stationed his own warriors in each region), or impose direct control through officials from the center (later Rome replaced local kings with Roman officials).

    Case study: the Jewish Revolt, 66–73 CE

    Causes: heavy taxes plus a failure to protect the local population from Greeks. Escalation: riots grew into full rebellion. Result: Jerusalem destroyed, roughly a million deaths, and the fortress of Masada fell — ending in mass suicide. Use this when asked what happened when imperial control broke down, or what the limits of toleration were.

    Toleration and violence, together

    Empires practiced some toleration to satisfy local customs and beliefs — but used violence if a people proved disloyal. The two are not opposites; they are two settings on the same instrument.

    • Persian Empire: broad toleration, cultural freedom allowed as long as loyalty was maintained.
    • Roman Empire: treated conquered peoples as inferiors. A few wealthy provincials gained citizenship; most faced harsh punishment.

    Long-term impact: these same three tools carried forward into European colonial empires in the 18th and 19th centuries — religion and efficient administration used alongside military force, as in the British Empire.

    Mesopotamia — The Land of Firsts

    Article: Mesopotamia. The unit's geography case study. The single most important idea here: Mesopotamia's environment explains its politics, its wealth, and its collapse. Everything loops back to the two rivers.

    Mesopotamia at a glance
      
    WhereBetween the Tigris and Euphrates rivers, flowing from the Zagros Mountains to the Persian Gulf — modern Iraq. The name means "land between the rivers." Part of the Fertile Crescent.
    Two halvesSouthern Mesopotamia = Sumer (city-states Eridu, Ur, Uruk). Northern Mesopotamia = Akkad. Independent politically, but shared a language and culture.
    Key datesLarge city-states form c. 3800 BCE; writing c. 3400 BCE; Sargon's Akkadian Empire c. 2334–2279 BCE; conquered by Alexander the Great 331 BCE.
    WritingCuneiform — wedge-shaped marks pressed into clay tablets.
    Political shapeIndependent city-states → the first empire.
    Ended byEnvironmental strain → famine → rebellion → invasion. A repeating cycle, not a single fall.

    Geography

    Mesopotamian geography: strengths and weaknesses
    CharacteristicProCon
    Water sourceTwo large rivers; snowmelt from the Zagros Mountains floods them annually, depositing silt that makes soil fertile.Flooding was unpredictable and destructive — no reliable calendar for it.
    Land / climateFlat land is easy to farm and easy to move across; irrigation and canals extended farmland far beyond the riverbanks, producing surpluses of wheat and barley.Semi-arid (dry). Irrigating a dry, flat plain causes salinization — salt building up in the soil until harvests fail.

    The chain that makes Mesopotamia make sense

    Fertile soil → surplus crops → surplus must be stored, distributed and defended → someone has to manage irrigation, canals and walls → kings and city-states → competition over water and land between city-states → armies → and because there are no natural barriers, outsiders can take it all whenever the harvest fails.

    Trade

    • Exported: wheat, barley, reeds, clay pottery — the products of surplus farming and abundant clay.
    • Imported: metals, wood, and precious stones like lapis lazuli, from societies in Egypt, India and Afghanistan. Mesopotamia traded food for the raw materials its own land could not supply.
    • Sargon standardized weights and measures and built roads, which made trade flourish — a direct example of a ruler investing in commerce.
    • The Standard of Ur (lapis from Afghanistan, shells from the Persian Gulf, marble from India) is the artifact that proves the reach of these networks.
    • Writing itself began as trade recordkeeping — tablets recording bushels of wheat — before it was ever used for literature.

    Religion

    • Polytheistic — many gods, tied to natural forces and to individual cities. Inanna was goddess of love and war; Nanna the moon god at Ur. There was even a goddess of beer.
    • Worship centered on the ziggurat, a massive stepped temple. High priests and priestesses lived in the ziggurat and performed the rituals that kept the gods appeased.
    • Rulers claimed to speak with the gods in order to win divine favor and protection for agriculture — and some kings claimed to be divine themselves. This is pattern R2D: religion legitimizing political power.
    • Religion also explained the environment (pattern NW): if flooding is unpredictable and the harvest can fail, gods who control water and weather need to be kept happy.
    • The Epic of Gilgamesh is religious as well as literary — a king's hero's journey to find the secret of eternal life, and the oldest surviving work of literature (c. 2100 BCE), written in Akkadian.

    Government

    • City-state: a self-governing state made up of a city and its surrounding territory. Each had its own king. Eridu, Ur and Uruk are the ones to name.
    • Kings existed because surplus food had to be managed and protected. Their jobs: direct large projects (irrigation systems, canals, roads, bridges, city walls), command soldiers against rival city-states, and claim to speak with the gods so that people accepted their authority.
    • City-states fought each other constantly over access to water and land.
    Sargon of Akkad, c. 2334–2279 BCE — the world's first empire

    He united the region by conquest; his empire stretched from the Mediterranean Sea to the Persian Gulf. His innovations are a standard test list:

    1. The first standing army (~5,000 soldiers). 2. The first postal system, to communicate with his officials. 3. Akkadian made the official language. 4. Roads. 5. Standardized weights and measures for fair commerce. He also appointed people he trusted to power — including making his daughter Enheduanna high priestess at Ur.

    The weaknesses: he needed that standing army to put down repeated rebellions. After his death, a famine weakened the empire; nomadic people from the Zagros Mountains defeated his dynasty by 2150 BCE and the empire broke back apart into city-states. But Sargon became the model ruler for later kings — Hammurabi of Babylon and Sargon II of Assyria.

    Special cultural features

    Mesopotamia is called the land of firsts. Know the list:

    Mesopotamian firsts
    FirstDetail

    LiteratureThe Epic of Gilgamesh, the first epic poem: a king's quest for immortality. Author unknown.

    Social classes

    Hierarchical and male-dominated, but with real movement between levels:

    The Mesopotamian social pyramid, top to bottom
    LevelWho
    1Kings, nobles, priests and priestesses. The king had a special relationship with the gods; some claimed divinity.
    2Merchants, military officers, scribes.
    3Farmers, soldiers, artisans — most people.
    4Enslaved people. Usually captured in war, though some sold themselves into slavery to pay debts — and people could buy their freedom and move up.

    Women of the lower classes worked as midwives, perfumers, brewers and tavern managers; some women, like Enheduanna, held real power at the top.

    Decline and transformation

    Mesopotamia never had one collapse — it had a repeating cycle, and the test may well ask you to describe it:

    1. Farming depends entirely on the rivers and irrigation.
    2. Uncontrolled flooding, or too much salt in the soil, ruins the harvest.
    3. Crop failure → famine.
    4. Famine weakens the king's authority and sparks rebellion.
    5. With no natural barriers, outsiders seize the moment of weakness and invade.

    The sequence of rulers to memorize: City-states → Akkadians → Babylonians → Assyrians → Persians → Alexander the Great (331 BCE).

    How we know

    Clay tablets — trade records, letters, hymns, the Epic of Gilgamesh — preserved by a dry climate. Plus the ziggurats, the Standard of Ur, and the stela of Sargon. Mesopotamia is one of the best-documented societies in the unit precisely because its writing material was fireproof, waterproof mud.

    Shang Dynasty China

    Article: Agrarian Shang Dynasty China and Early States. Ruled 1600–1046 BCE. The society where religion and government are the same institution — the king is both head of state and head shaman — and where a single unlooted tomb rewrote what historians thought about women's power.

    Shang China at a glance
      
    WhereNorthern China along the Huang He (Yellow River).
    When1600–1046 BCE — over 500 years.
    WritingPictograms carved on oracle bones — the ancestor of modern Chinese script.
    Key sitesErligang (modern Zhengzhou), walled city. Yinxu (modern Anyang), where Lady Fu Hao's intact tomb was found.
    Signature technologyBronze casting, plus early silk production and a 365-day solar calendar.
    Ended byOverthrown by the Zhou at the Battle of Muye, 1046 BCE.
    Why the Shang and not the Xia?

    Chinese historical texts describe an earlier Xia dynasty (c. 2070–1600 BCE), but there is no archaeological evidence for it. The Shang is called China's first dynasty because it is the first that is confirmed by physical evidence: walled cities, tombs, bronze artifacts, and oracle bones, backed by later Chinese histories written in the 5th–1st centuries BCE.

    This is a question about method as much as content. The lesson: historians distinguish between what texts claim and what evidence confirms.

    Geography

    • Early farming communities grew along the Huang He (Yellow River) valley, which supplied fresh water, fertile soil, and transportation — the same river-valley pattern as Mesopotamia, Egypt and the Indus.
    • The land supported grains and other crops, with some rice in the north — but nothing like the full-scale rice agriculture of southern China later.
    • To the north and west lived pastoralists, whom the settled Chinese called "barbarians." That relationship matters twice: pastoralists were a military threat (hence walled cities) and a source of technology (the horse-drawn chariot came from the steppe).
    • Cities were walled for defense. Erligang's walls were about 10 m (32 ft) high and 20 m (65 ft) thick, enclosing more than 3 km².

    Trade

    • Cowry shells found in tombs prove trade with coastal regions far from the Shang heartland. Lady Fu Hao's tomb alone held 6,900 cowry shells.
    • Bronze-wheeled chariots show contact with the steppe peoples to the west, who were skilled with horses and chariots — technology transfer, not just goods.
    • The Shang exported what they were best at: bronze work and early silk textiles, both highly desirable to neighboring communities.
    • East Asian exchange networks therefore extended well beyond the walled cities the Shang directly controlled, reaching nearby farming and pastoralist areas.
    • Oracle bones and other records tracked farming harvests and surpluses, trade balances and debts, and taxation — writing used for economic administration.

    Religion

    Animism
    The belief that objects, places and creatures possess spirits. The natural world is alive with spiritual force.
    Ancestor worship
    When a family member died they joined the spirit world, and the living called on their ancestors for help and guidance. This makes family lineage a religious institution, not just a social one — which is exactly why dynastic rule felt natural.
    Oracle bones
    The central practice. A shaman or diviner scratched a question onto an ox scapula or turtle shell, heated it until it cracked, and read the cracks as the answer. The question, the asker, and the answer were then engraved on the bone.
    Sacrifice
    Rituals and sacrifices were offered to gods, spirits and ancestors. Some servants or enslaved people were buried with the elite — Fu Hao's tomb held 16 servants and 6 dogs.
    Afterlife
    Tombs were stocked with tools, weapons, instruments and beauty accessories for use in the spiritual realm — strong evidence of belief in a life after death.

    Why oracle bones matter so much: they are simultaneously the best evidence for Shang religion, the earliest surviving Chinese writing, and a record of what the elite actually worried about — harvests, military strategy, even the gender of a royal child. One artifact type answering three different question categories.

    Government

    • Dynasty: rulers who pass the right to rule down through their family line.
    • The Shang king held a dual role — political and spiritual leader. He made laws, commanded the military, and performed sacred rituals communicating with spirits. His right to rule came from the gods of ancient China.
    • Toward the end of the dynasty, the king became the chief shaman himself, which concentrated religious and political authority in one person even more tightly.
    • Wives and consorts could hold real power — Lady Fu Hao commanded armies and conducted sacrifices.
    • Rule was enforced through walled cities, a standing military, and a class of bureaucrats just below the king.

    Social classes

    Shang social structure, top to bottom
    LevelWho
    1Kings and the wealthy elite
    2Military and bureaucrats
    3Artisans and skilled craftspeople
    4Peasants / farmers — most of the population
    5Enslaved people and servants — some buried alongside the elite, which is how archaeologists identified their status

    Special cultural features

    Bronze casting
    The signature Shang technology. Bronze tools, weapons and ritual vessels, produced in dedicated foundries; thousands of bronze artifacts have been excavated. Jade, bone and stone crafts were made alongside.
    Stamped earth
    A construction technique — soil compacted into a solid foundation to support wooden buildings, the ancient equivalent of pouring a concrete slab.
    Walled cities
    Founded by the kings and walled against invasion. Erligang's walls: ~10 m high, 20 m thick.
    Writing
    Pictograms that closely resemble modern Chinese characters — an unbroken writing tradition over 3,000 years old.
    Astronomy & calendar
    Recorded eclipses and solstices and built a 365-day solar calendar — more accurate than a 354-day lunar calendar, and crucially it told farmers when to plant and harvest. Science in the service of agriculture.
    Silk
    Early silk textile production — the good that would eventually name the Silk Road.
    Music
    Flutes and drums found at Shang sites show organized cultural life beyond survival and ritual.
    Workshops
    Most craftwork was done at home, but as cities grew, workshops were founded and people moved to urban areas to work — a textbook example of job specialization drawing people into cities.
    Lady Fu Hao — the highest-value example in the unit

    A consort of King Wu Ding, c. 1250 BCE. She was a military general who led her own troops into battle and a spiritual leader who conducted sacrifices and petitioned ancestors. She is named on close to 200 oracle bones.

    Her tomb at Yinxu was found intact in 1976 — never looted, which is why it is so valuable. Contents: 16 servants and 6 dogs (sacrificial burials), ~500 bronze objects including 130 weapons, over 700 jade items, 5 ivory pieces, over 500 bone ornaments, and 6,900 cowry shells.

    What it proves: (1) unusual female power and prestige was possible in Shang society, though it was exceptional rather than typical; (2) the weapons corroborate the oracle-bone claim that she was a general — two independent evidence types agreeing; (3) the cowry shells prove long-distance trade; (4) the human and animal sacrifices show what the Shang believed about the afterlife and about the status of enslaved people.

    Decline: the Mandate of Heaven

    The Shang ruled for over 500 years before the Zhou, a powerful group from the plains of eastern China, overthrew the last Shang king at the Battle of Muye in 1046 BCE.

    King Wu of the Zhou justified the rebellion by claiming the Shang king had lost the Mandate of Heaven (tian ming): the king was corrupt and focused on his own pleasure at the expense of his people, so he had lost his virtue and therefore his right to rule.

    This is one of the most important ideas in the unit because of what it does: it makes divine right conditional. A ruler is chosen by heaven, but heaven can withdraw its choice — so rebellion against a bad ruler is not treason but the correction of a moral failure. The Zhou went on to rule for almost 800 years, the longest dynasty in Chinese history.

    How we know

    Oracle bones (tens of thousands of them), bronze artifacts, walled city ruins, and tombs — above all the unlooted Tomb 5 of Lady Fu Hao at Yinxu. Written Chinese histories from the 5th–1st centuries BCE (The Book of Documents, The Bamboo Annals, The Records of the Grand Historian) were composed centuries after the fact, so archaeology is what confirms them.

    Egypt & Nubia

    Article: Nubia, Ancient Egypt, and Agrarian Societies. The unit's religion case study. Note the pairing: the test says "Egypt/Nubia," so Nubia is not optional background — you need both, and you need the relationship between them.

    Egypt and Nubia at a glance
     Nubia (A-Group)Ancient Egypt
    WhereSouth of Upper Egypt, modern Sudan, between the first and second cataracts of the Nile.The Nile valley — Upper Egypt (south, upriver) and Lower Egypt (north, the Delta).
    Whenc. 3800–2900 BCEPredynastic 6000–3200 BCE; Protodynastic 3200–3000 BCE; Early Dynastic 3000–2700 BCE
    Evidence~200 sites, mostly cemeteries plus some housingTombs, temples, the Narmer Palette, hieroglyphic texts — though many early sites lie below the modern water table
    SignificanceOne of the earliest farming societies in the Indo-Mediterranean worldUnified c. 3200–3000 BCE under Narmer; becomes an ancient superpower

    Geography

    • The Nile is the longest river in the world and it sustained life along a narrow ribbon through desert. Three reasons people settled along it: fresh water, fertile soil from annual flooding, and a transport route for trade — plus irrigation for farming.
    • Climate change drove settlement. As the region dried out, fewer resources were available away from the river, so people were pushed toward the Nile. The shift from foraging to farming here was gradual — and unusually, domestication of animals came before full farming societies.
    • Settlement pattern: the closer to the river, the more farming. On the rural outskirts people mixed small-plot farming, foraging, herding and hunting, and were more self-sufficient; near the river were the power centers.
    • A cataract is a stretch of shallow, rocky rapids in the Nile. Cataracts made boat travel difficult, so they acted as natural borders and defensive barriers — and as chokepoints that controlled trade. Nubia is defined by its position between the first and second cataracts.
    • Unlike Mesopotamia, Egypt's flooding was regular and predictable, and it was protected by deserts on both sides. Compare that to Mesopotamia's unpredictable floods and total lack of barriers — this is one of the strongest geography contrasts available for the extended response.

    Trade

    • Nubia's position was its economic identity: it sat on the flow of goods from inner Africa to the Mediterranean — especially ivory.
    • Nubian sites contain gold, copper, pottery, flint, stone tools and grindstones — some local, some from Egypt and beyond.
    • Lapis lazuli appears in Nubian artifacts, including a bone figurine of a woman. The nearest source was Afghanistan — thousands of miles away. That single stone proves Nubia was plugged into very long-distance exchange, and marks the objects containing it as high-status.
    • Lower Egypt had fertile soil and access to Mediterranean and Southwest Asian networks, which is why it grew fastest in settlements, population and power.
    • Some Egyptian crops may have originated in the Fertile Crescent — agricultural knowledge itself traveling along trade routes.
    • Egypt's export identity across the region: grain. Nubia and the upper Nile: iron.

    Religion

    This is the section the class spent the most time on. Learn the artifacts, and learn which of the five patterns each one demonstrates.

    Egyptian religious artifacts and what each demonstrates

    Nubian and Egyptian religion compared

    • Both buried their dead facing west with offerings to guide them into the afterlife — shared burial customs pointing to shared beliefs.
    • Both revered the goddess Isis in a similar way.
    • Abydos was a necropolis (a city of the dead) shared by Nubians and Naqada Egyptians, and later chosen as the burial place for Egyptian royalty — physical proof of a shared ritual culture.
    • Nubian bone figurines inlaid with lapis lazuli were likely fertility or religious symbols, buried only with people of high standing.

    Government

    • Before unification: the Scorpion Kings ruled Upper Egypt. Scorpion I took control of the whole of Upper Egypt around 3200 BCE.
    • Narmer (possibly the same person as the later-named Menes) unified Upper and Lower Egypt. The Narmer Palette, a carved stone tablet from c. 3000 BCE, depicts him conquering both lands and uniting them — and it is what caused historians to revise the older Menes timeline. Unification marks the start of the Early Dynastic Period.
    • Pharaohs were god-kings — simultaneously divine and political rulers. This is the cleanest example of R2D in the unit, and it made rebellion close to blasphemy.
    • Unification meant centralized power, the emergence of Egypt as a superpower, and the spread of writing for administration.
    • In Nubia, there was no unified kingdom in the A-Group period, but high-status burials with cattle, pottery, beads, ivory, seals and maceheads show hierarchy and power. A macehead is the ornamental head of a mace or staff — carried by people in positions of authority, so it is evidence of rank.

    Special cultural features

    Two scripts
    Hieratic for everyday recordkeeping; hieroglyphics reserved for religious purposes. Both spread after unification. Egyptian and Nubian scripts share traits, hinting at common origins or a shared language with regional dialects.
    Duality
    The organizing idea of Egyptian thought: reality is made of balanced opposing pairs — Upper and Lower Egypt, life and afterlife, male and female. Everything from politics to religion is structured this way.
    Status of women
    Because duality treated male and female as complementary, Egyptian women reached a status unknown in most other ancient societies: they owned land and businesses, held important religious and agricultural roles, and received equal pay for equal work — something most industrialized countries still have not achieved.
    Nubian women
    Also unusually well regarded. Female burials contain important grave goods. Women were seen as givers of life and often experts in agriculture, and led rituals of birth, fertility, death and rebirth.
    Mummification
    Preserving the body (and its organs, in canopic jars) because bodily preservation was required for eternal life. It follows directly from the belief system, not from vanity.
    Monumental architecture
    Pyramids, temples and necropolises — huge structures housing the dead, built by a state able to command enormous surplus labor.
    Nubian housing
    Stone houses, storage pits, grindstones — a settled farming community with the ability to store surplus.
    Why studying ancient Egypt is difficult

    A near-certain short answer. Three reasons: (1) Egyptian history is enormously long — the pyramids, King Tut and Cleopatra are separated by thousands of years, so "ancient Egypt" is not one thing. (2) Many of the earliest sites now lie under the current water level, making excavation extremely hard. (3) The Predynastic period left no writing, so the origins of the society are reconstructed from objects alone.

    What helps archaeologists: the dry climate preserves organic material; Egyptians buried goods with the dead, creating sealed time capsules; hieroglyphic and hieratic writing can be read; and monumental architecture and art survived above ground. The Narmer Palette is the model case — one carved object that reset the timeline of unification.

    The Nubia–Egypt relationship, in one paragraph

    Nubia lay south of Upper Egypt and was not simply a colony or a rival. The two were connected and mutually influential: similar artifacts, similar written symbols, similar artistic features, similar ideas about rulers, shared burial customs (west-facing, with offerings), a shared reverence for Isis, a shared necropolis at Abydos, and established trade networks in which Nubia moved ivory and iron from inner Africa toward the Mediterranean. Nubia was also one of the earliest farming societies in the whole Indo-Mediterranean world — so it is not a junior partner in the story.

    How we know

    Nubia: almost 200 excavated sites, mostly cemeteries — which is a limitation worth naming, since a society known mainly through its graves is known mainly through its dead and its elite. Egypt: tombs and grave goods, temple carvings and inscriptions, the Narmer Palette, papyrus texts like the Book of the Dead, and a century of excavation.

    Olmec & Chavín

    Article: Ancient Agrarian Societies: The Olmec and Chavín. Two American societies, roughly 5,500 km (3,500 miles) apart, treated as one topic. Neither left written records, so everything here is inferred from objects — which is itself a likely test question.

    The two American societies compared
     OlmecChavín
    Datesc. 1600–350 BCEc. 900–250 BCE
    WhereMesoamerica — along the Coatzacoalcos River near the Gulf of Mexico; modern Veracruz and Tabasco, MexicoSouth America — where the Mosna and Huachecsa rivers meet, on the Peruvian Pacific coast in the Andes Mountains region; modern Peru
    Main sitesSan Lorenzo (largest), La Venta, Tres Zapotes, Laguna de los CerrosChavín de Huántar — the temple complex
    PopulationPerhaps 20,000 at its height~1,000 acres supporting perhaps 2,000–3,000 people
    WritingNoneNone — we do not even know what they called themselves; "Chavín" is the Spanish name for the temple ruins
    Led byKings or chiefs using political, economic and religious authorityShaman-priests held the most authority

    Geography

    • Both settled along river valleys, like every other society in this unit — the Olmec near the Gulf of Mexico, the Chavín where two Andean rivers meet.
    • The Olmec mixed farming with foraging methods like hunting and fishing. Foraging communities had lived in the region since at least 2500 BCE.
    • The Chavín exploited two elevations at once: quinoa in the lowlands near the river, potatoes up in the mountains. Farming two ecological zones is a distinctly Andean adaptation and a good specific detail to cite.
    • Volcanic geology mattered. Basalt for the Olmec heads came from the Sierra de los Tuxtlas Mountains; obsidian, the region's substitute for metal, is volcanic glass.
    • Geography also destroyed them: climate change and volcanic eruptions may have ruined Olmec farmland, and overuse of surrounding land is one theory for the Chavín decline.

    Trade

    • The Olmec's materials are the evidence. Basalt and stone were local, but jade was imported from the south and obsidian from the north. Foreign materials appearing constantly in Olmec art proves extensive regional trade networks.
    • Remember the Mesoamerican constraint: no pack animals, no sailing ships, rivers poor for long hauls. Goods moved on human backs and in large canoes along coastal routes, so only high-value goods traveled: obsidian, jade, cacao, salt, cotton, feathers.
    • The Chavín domesticated llamas and used them to transport goods throughout the region and beyond. Goods originating far outside Chavín territory have been found hundreds of miles from its center, placing them in a larger interregional trade network. Note the contrast with Mesoamerica: the Andes did have a pack animal.
    • Trade moved style and belief, not just objects: nearby societies copied Chavín artistic style in their own pottery and sculpture, and later Mesoamerican societies — the Maya and Aztec — share enough with Olmec art and culture that they appear to have adopted aspects of it.
    • Increased pottery production after 500 BCE at Chavín signals increased food production — more jars were needed to carry and store surplus. Pottery counts as an economic indicator.

    Religion

    • Olmec gods blended human and animal forms, both male and female — distinct from the individual human portraits of their rulers.
    • Human sacrifice and ritual bloodletting. Archaeologists have found daggers and other bloodletting tools. In bloodletting, a shaman cut a cheek, earlobe or tongue with an obsidian dagger, bone or stingray barb; the blood was placed on cotton and burned to summon the gods.
    • The ballgame, played with a rubber ball on a ball court, was both sport and ritual — the losing team could be sacrificed.
    • Chavín de Huántar is a temple of staircases, archways and maze-like underground corridors. Processions entered and watched a shaman conduct ceremonies on platforms in the complex. Bloodletting was performed on stone slabs there.
    • Shamans induced trance states by ingesting mushrooms, coca leaves and cactus juice.
    • Chavín temple imagery mixes real and mythical animals: jaguars, snakes, eagles, and a winged crocodile.
    • The shared analytical point: in both societies, religion may have functioned as social control through a mixture of spiritual belief and fear. That is the article's own interpretation and a strong line to use in a short answer — but flag it as an interpretation, because the evidence is indirect.

    Government

    • Olmec: archaeologists believe central rulers — kings or chiefs — governed the cities using political, economic and religious authority together. The colossal heads are the strongest evidence: each depicts a male head with distinctive individual features, so they represent specific rulers, not gods (gods were shown as human-animal blends).
    • Olmec cities were reserved for kings and elites and used mainly for ritual. Most people lived in rural areas as farmers and craftspeople — the city was a ceremonial and administrative center, not where the population lived.
    • Different Olmec cities rose to prominence and then declined at different times, which may mean ritual centers were relocated for some reason.
    • Chavín: organized hierarchically with shaman-priests at the top — likely a religious central authority — but honestly, we do not know how it was organized politically. Notably, there are no defensive structures and no weapons of war, which suggests authority rested on religion rather than on military force.
    • Nobody lived at the Chavín temple. No hearths, no trash, no dwellings — the absence of ordinary domestic debris is how archaeologists concluded it was a shared ritual site visited by several settlements rather than a city. Dwellings and farms were outside the complex.
    The colossal heads — a whole answer in one object

    Carved from basalt (volcanic rock), weighing about 20 tons each, quarried in the Sierra de los Tuxtlas Mountains and moved 60–70 km (37–43 miles) to Olmec sites — with no pack animals and no wheeled transport.

    What that tells you: a society able to move a 20-ton stone dozens of miles by human labor alone must be able to command an enormous coordinated workforce, feed it from agricultural surplus, and possess the authority to make it happen. The heads are evidence of political power, economic surplus and social hierarchy simultaneously — which is why they show up on tests so often.

    Special cultural features

    Olmec colossal heads
    20-ton basalt portraits of individual rulers — the most recognizable Olmec artifact.
    Rubber
    The Olmec were among the first to extract sap from the rubber tree and turn it into a stretchy, bouncing material. The Aztecs later named them "Olmec," meaning "rubber people."
    The ballgame
    Played with a rubber ball on excavated ball courts. Sport and ritual at once, with sacrifice as a possible stake.
    Olmec crops
    Maize, cotton, squash, beans, sweet potatoes, manioc and rubber.
    Olmec materials
    Clay and stone, plus jade (green gemstone) and obsidian (volcanic glass) — used in freestanding and relief sculpture, pottery, masks and ritual tools.
    Chavín metalwork
    Skilled soldering of gold to create crowns, masks and jewelry — joining metal by melting it enough to reshape and attach.
    Chavín textiles
    Woven cotton made into wall hangings and clothing.
    Chavín architecture
    The temple's staircases, archways and underground corridors, expanded after 500 BCE — possibly to hold larger crowds for ceremonies.

    Decline

    • Olmec (ended c. 350 BCE): the leading theories are climate change and volcanic eruptions ruining farmland in parts of the region. Because there are no written records, population figures and causes are assumptions based on land area and artifacts.
    • Chavín (ended c. 250 BCE): scholars are unsure. Possibilities include environmental change such as overuse of the surrounding land, or the religious site itself losing importance.
    • Both are honest "we don't know" cases — and saying so, with the candidate explanations, is a better answer than inventing certainty.

    How we know

    Only physical evidence: sculpture, pottery, masks, ritual tools, ball courts, temple architecture, imported jade and obsidian, bloodletting implements, and the absence of things (no hearths at Chavín de Huántar, no weapons, no defensive walls). Absence of evidence is used as evidence here — a sophisticated point worth making if a question asks how historians study societies without writing.

    Aksum

    Article: Ancient Agrarian Societies: Aksum. The unit's trade case study. By the 3rd century CE ancient writers counted Aksum among the "Big 4" world powers alongside Rome, Persia and China. The whole story is: location plus trade turned a small settlement into an empire — and then overreach and plague took it apart.

    Aksum at a glance
      
    WhereThe "Northern Horn" of East Africa — modern Ethiopia and Eritrea, on the Red Sea. At its height it controlled north Ethiopia, parts of Sudan, and southern Arabia (notably Yemen).
    Peak3rd century CE — one of the four great powers of the world.
    Turning point30 BCE: Rome under Augustus conquers Egypt, shifting trade toward the Red Sea and putting Aksum at the center of it.
    Key portAdulis — the market town where goods from the African interior entered the network.
    ReligionAnimism → astronomical gods → Christianity as state religion, 4th century CE.
    Ended byOverexpansion in Yemen, elite infighting, and the Justinianic Plague (c. 541 CE).

    Geography

    • East Africa is the cradle of our species — the homeland of every human being on the planet.
    • Why farming came late here: the region had abundant resources, so people kept foraging and herding rather than settling to farm. Some communities were reluctant to switch, since farming meant a less healthy, more work-filled life. This is the unit's best counterexample to "farming is progress."
    • They domesticated ensete (a plant in the banana family) as early as 3000 BCE, and foraged animal hides, bird feathers, myrrh for perfume, and obsidian to trade with Egypt.
    • By 2000 BCE most Northern Horn communities were semi-nomadic, mixing foraging, farming and pastoralism. Copper and bronze were rare, so they used stone tools and later iron.
    • Location is the whole story: Aksum sat between two major trade networks — the Mediterranean/Roman world and the Indian Ocean — on the Red Sea. Its main income was still agriculture and herding (cereal grains, cattle, sheep, goats), but geography made it rich.

    Trade

    • Collective learning: knowledge of farming filtered down from Egypt and Southwest Asia along trade networks, which is how agriculture spread across Africa. Aksum's architecture and culture were likewise shaped by contact with Arabia and Egypt. Ideas travel on trade routes is the unit's recurring line, and Aksum is where it is clearest.
    • 30 BCE — the pivot. Rome conquered Egypt, and trade shifted from the Persian Gulf and overland Asian routes toward the Red Sea. Aksum became the hub of overseas trade between the Roman Empire and India.
    • Goods handled: ivory, gold, emeralds, silk, spices, crops, salt, exotic animals, perfume and manufactured goods, moving between India and the Mediterranean. At Adulis, African interior goods were exchanged for wine, olive oil, Roman and Indian coins, and bronze lamps.
    • Aksum built a navy to patrol the Red Sea and protect its trade routes — the clearest case in the unit of a ruler spending military power to defend commerce. Naval power also let them transport troops and expand.
    • Aksum minted its own coinage, with each dynasty etched onto gold coins. Those coins have been found from Rome to Persia to India — direct archaeological proof of the network's size.
    • Trade wealth drove urbanization and job specialization, and paid for monumental architecture, with palaces as the largest structures.

    Religion

    • Began animistic — belief in many spirits inhabiting the natural world.
    • Formalized into gods tied to astronomy: Mahrem (king of the gods, god of war), Astar (Venus), Behēr (god of the sea) — honored with animal sacrifices and rituals. A god of the sea in a sea-trading empire is not an accident.
    • The elite adopted aspects of Greek culture, including language and religious ideas — cultural borrowing arriving with commerce.
    • Christianity arrived along the trade routes and became the state religion in the 4th century CE, linking Aksum to the Roman Empire, which had also just adopted it. A Greco-Phoenician tutor named Frumentius is said to have taught the royal children, influenced the family to convert, and become the first Bishop of Aksum.
    • Conversion of the whole empire was gradual, over decades, and local customs were folded into the Christian faith — a good example of religious change being blended rather than imposed overnight.

    Government

    • Unified under kings and expanded by military conquest into an empire controlling a key trade region.
    • The Aksumite ruler was the "king of kings." Smaller kingdoms kept their own kings but paid tribute — a required "gift" of goods to a governing authority.
    • How rebellion was prevented: the king stationed warriors in each region, and he and his administrators collected the tribute in person and met with the regional kings, who pledged their support. That is a textbook example of controlling the periphery through local rulers plus a garrison.
    • Adopting Christianity as a state religion was also a governing act — it unified subjects and aligned Aksum with Rome.
    Stelae — how a monument proves hierarchy

    Aksumite elites were buried in elaborate tombs marked by huge carved stone pillars called stelae — one built under King Ezana stands 23 meters high. They carried intricate carvings and also commemorated victories, regions of the kingdom, and great events.

    The argument: bigger stela = higher status. A society that ranks its dead by the size of their monuments is a society with a clear social hierarchy — and the stelae are cited as evidence for exactly that, alongside the roles they imply: bureaucrats, priests, soldiers, merchants and artisans.

    Special cultural features

    • Stelae — monumental carved pillars marking tombs, up to 23 m tall.
    • Its own coinage in gold, stamped by dynasty — one of very few African states of this era to mint coins.
    • Greek influence on elite language and culture; Christianity from the 4th century CE.
    • Iron working, possibly developed independently or adopted from other African societies.
    • Rapid urban growth — the capital expanded so fast there was no master plan for its layout or walls, which is itself evidence of how suddenly trade wealth arrived.
    • An earlier kingdom, D'mt (peaked 10th–5th centuries BCE), preceded Aksum, developed sustained farming through Egyptian trade contacts, and exported intricate stone jewelry. When D'mt fell, smaller iron-working kingdoms filled the space.

    Decline

    Three causes stacking on top of each other:

    1. Overexpansion. Military campaigns to control Yemen in the 6th century CE drained the treasury — "its desire to expand was part of its own downfall."
    2. Internal division. The Aksumite elite were fighting among themselves at the same time.
    3. The Justinianic Plague, c. 541 CE — almost certainly the same disease that caused the Black Death 800 years later.

    Aksum held on because of trade profits, but its territory and population shrank. Note the shape of this: the same trade that built it was the only thing keeping it alive at the end.

    How we know

    Aksumite coins found from Rome to Persia to India, the stelae and elite tombs, the ruins of Aksum and Adulis, and Greco-Roman written sources that begin mentioning Aksum in the 1st century CE. Aksum is unusual in this unit for having both strong archaeology and outside written testimony.

    Nok

    Article: Ancient Agrarian Societies: Nok Society. West Africa, in and around modern northern Nigeria. Nok is the unit's argument about evidence itself: a complex society with no cities, no kings, no palaces and no writing — which forces you to ask what "complex" actually means.

    Nok at a glance
      
    WhereNorthern Nigeria, around the town of Jos, West Africa.
    WhenRoughly the 6th century BCE to the 6th century CE. Iron furnaces dated to 280 BCE, and later work pushed some as early as 500 BCE. The terracotta style dies out around 100 CE.
    Discovered byArchaeologist Bernard Fagg, who found hundreds of terracotta sculptures — and then, unexpectedly, iron furnaces.
    Settlement typeNo cities. Many dispersed settlements, each probably centered on an extended family.
    WritingNone. History in this region was often maintained through oral tradition.
    Possible descendantsMay be ancestors of the modern Yoruba culture of Nigeria.
    Why Nok is in this unit at all

    We usually learn that Ghana and Mali were the first complex societies in West Africa — but those are recorded only after the 10th century CE. Agriculture in West Africa began possibly 4,000 years ago. That would leave a 3,000-year gap between the start of farming and the first complex society, which is implausible.

    Why the gap exists in the record, not in reality: (1) later invasions and conflict destroyed records; (2) history here was carried by oral tradition rather than writing; (3) what we know of Ghana and Mali comes largely from North African Muslim writers who crossed the Sahara by the 10th century and wrote down what they saw — so societies before outside observers arrived simply went unrecorded. Archaeological remains fill the gap, and Nok is the best example.

    Geography

    • Before farming, people lived in the Sahara — which was then a vast grassland, not desert. They herded cattle and ate wild grasses.
    • As the world dried, they were driven into river valleys and began cultivating the wild grasses they had been gathering. Same causal chain as Egypt: climate change → concentration near water → farming.
    • By 2000 BCE, pearl millet, barley and cattle were sustaining dense populations.
    • The land supplied the two raw materials Nok is known for: iron ore and clay for terracotta.

    Trade

    • Be careful and honest here: we believe Nok was one of many complex societies in the region and that these societies may have traded with each other — but we do not have a lot of evidence to prove it. Saying that clearly is a stronger answer than inventing trade routes.
    • What we can say: iron production was concentrated at dedicated furnace sites away from settlements, and terracotta may have been made by specialists — and specialized production implies goods moving between households and communities.
    • The stylistic consistency of terracotta across the region is itself a form of connection: a shared style spread over a wide area means people and ideas were moving.

    Religion

    • Archaeologists have found sites that appear to be for religious or spiritual ritual: places where one large sculpture was intentionally left or buried in the ground, suggesting a marker or shrine. Sometimes five or six similar sculptures appear together.
    • Crucially, these sites are almost always far away from settlements — which suggests they were shared locations for worship or ritual, used by multiple communities rather than owned by one.
    • They may have played a role in governing: people might have gathered there to make agreements or hear disputes. Religion and law occupying the same site is a genuinely different model from the temple-plus-palace pattern elsewhere.

    Government

    • There is no evidence of kingship, palaces, or even temples that would signify a ruling class. Memorize that sentence — it is the single most distinctive fact about Nok, and the contrast that makes it valuable in an extended response.
    • Society was built on settlements centered on extended families, each with its own farm and cattle.
    • The plausible model is decentralized authority: shared ritual sites where agreements were made and disputes heard, rather than a palace issuing commands.
    • There may still have been status differences — expert blacksmiths may have held a different class status, and a specialist guild or class for terracotta may have existed, though not all experts agree.

    Special cultural features

    Terracotta sculpture
    Hundreds of brownish-red pottery sculptures in a consistent style: human figures with long heads, almond-shaped eyes and elaborate hairstyles — hairstyles somewhat like those worn by some Nigerians today. They depict common human experience: love, sickness, music and war, including a man and woman kneeling and embracing. Also jewelry and useful pots.
    Independent iron smelting
    The big one. Fagg dated charcoal inside Nok iron furnaces to 280 BCE, later work to perhaps 500 BCE. At the time, scholars believed iron technology was invented only once, in Eurasia, and reached Africa much later. Archaeologists have since shown the people of Nok may have developed iron smelting on their own.
    Stone and iron together
    Stone and iron tools were in use at the same time — technologies overlap rather than replacing each other cleanly, which complicates any simple "ages of progress" story.
    Furnace sites
    Many furnaces — enough to suggest a dense population — located away from settlements and ritual sites. Some iron may have been made in households, but most was produced at these dedicated sites.
    Slag
    The waste left over from smelting metal. Metal slag found in settlements is how archaeologists know ironworking happened there.
    Division of labor
    Grain-grinding stones and tools in every house show most work was agricultural. Women likely did most of the work of turning crops into food; terracotta figures depict men working with iron — an inference drawn from art, which is worth flagging as an inference.

    Decline

    We do not know what happened to the Nok. Their terracotta style seems to die out around 100 CE. They may be ancestors of the modern Yoruba of Nigeria. This is an appropriate place to say that the honest answer is uncertainty — and to note that we are constantly learning more about this era in African history.

    How we know

    Only archaeology: terracotta sculptures and their distribution, iron furnaces and radiocarbon-dated charcoal inside them, slag in settlements, grinding stones in houses, and the placement of ritual sites relative to settlements. No texts, no outside observers.

    The takeaway to use in an essay

    Nok proves that a society can be technologically sophisticated — independently smelting iron, producing a refined artistic tradition, supporting a dense population — without cities, kings, monumental architecture or writing. If a question asks how the type of available evidence shapes what we know, Nok is your answer: our picture of the ancient world is biased toward societies that wrote things down and built in stone, and that bias is a feature of the record, not of the past.

    Indus River Valley

    Article: Indus River Valley and Agrarian Societies. Modern Pakistan and northern India. The society with the best urban engineering in the unit and the least visible rulers — no palace, no central temple, and a script nobody can read.

    Indus River Valley at a glance
      
    WhereThe Indus River valley — modern Pakistan and northern India.
    Whenc. 3500–1700 BCE; society at its widest extent 2800–1900 BCE; trade peak 2600–1900 BCE.
    Great citiesHarappa and Mohenjo-daro, both in modern Pakistan, about 400 miles apart and strikingly similar.
    Population40,000–60,000 per city — and the area these culturally connected cities covered was much larger than Mesopotamia's city-states or contemporary Egypt.
    WritingIndus script — still undeciphered. Found on thousands of stamp seals and small tablets.
    RediscoveredBy archaeologists in the late 19th century; substantial digs from the 1920s.

    Geography

    • A classic river-valley farming society: fresh water, fertile soil, irrigation, and a transport route.
    • Parts of the cities were built on mounds, which protected against flooding and may also have served as defensive positions. Deliberate engineering against the environment, rather than just accepting it.
    • With 40,000–60,000 people per city, farming was an essential priority — the cities could not exist without a large agricultural base feeding them.
    • Geography also ended them: a river changed course away from Mohenjo-daro, and climate change and shifting tectonic plates are the leading explanations for the decline.

    Trade

    • Production and distribution of goods appear to have been the top priority of this society — a striking contrast with societies organized around a palace or a temple.
    • Both cities began as small agrarian communities that also produced handcrafted goods like beads and metalwork, and grew into major trading centers. As their wealth grew, so did their size — more mounds were added for production and housing.
    • Roads built for wheeled carts, with some expanded to two lanes to handle more traffic. Infrastructure designed around commerce.
    • Imports: lapis lazuli, gold, silver and tin found at Harappa, arriving from modern Afghanistan and Iran.
    • Exports: Harappan pottery, seals and weights have been found in Mesopotamia and other contemporary societies — two-way evidence of a working long-distance network.
    • Weights — objects used on balance scales to measure goods — are direct evidence of standardized, regulated exchange.
    Stamp seals — the signature artifact

    Thousands have been found: carvings in stone used to press impressions into soft clay that then hardened. More than 65% depict a unicorn; others show real animals. Archaeologists believe the animal motifs identified a wealthy family or person and were used to mark goods for trade — effectively a brand or signature.

    Pottery bearing the same markings supports the link between seals and trading families. Small tablets in Indus script may be a numbering or accounting system — and many were broken in half, which suggests business contracts where each party kept half as proof of the deal: a rock receipt.

    Religion

    • No central temple for the worship of gods has been found. One serious possibility is that religious belief was more personal here — practiced at home rather than through a state cult.
    • Recurring seal images that may be religious: a seated male figure wearing a horned headdress (possibly a god — we do not know), and a female figure strangling two tigers.
    • That second image matters for a different reason: a god-like figure strangling wild beasts also appears in the Mesopotamian Epic of Gilgamesh. Shared imagery across two distant societies is evidence that beliefs and ideas traveled along trade networks, exactly as goods did.
    • The Great Bath at Mohenjo-daro — built of mud brick, sealed with tar, filled from nearby wells — may have been used for religious purposes or ritual cleansing. Note the hedge: "may have been." Without a readable script, almost every religious claim about the Indus Valley is provisional.

    Government

    • Neither Harappa nor Mohenjo-daro had a central palace or temple structure. This is the defining fact.
    • The leading interpretation: the society may have been run by a number of wealthy families rather than a single central ruler — consistent with the stamp seals identifying prominent households.
    • Yet the cities show enormous coordinated planning: streets in a grid, city-wide wells, a city-wide drainage system. Someone organized that. So authority clearly existed — it simply did not advertise itself with palaces and god-kings.
    • That contrast is the most valuable thing Indus offers an extended response: Mesopotamia and Egypt made power visible; the Indus Valley made infrastructure visible instead.

    Special cultural features

    Grid-planned cities
    Streets laid out in a grid — planned before they were built, rather than growing organically. Compare Aksum, whose capital grew so fast it had no master plan at all.
    Water and sanitation
    Elaborate well systems delivering fresh water to inhabitants, and an advanced drainage system that carried sewage outside the city to the agricultural fields. Dealing with trash and sewage was a critical problem for every early society, and the Indus solved it best.
    The Great Bath
    At Mohenjo-daro: mud brick painted with tar as a waterproof sealer, supplied from nearby wells. Possibly for ritual cleansing.
    Flood mounds
    City sections raised on mounds for flood protection and possible defense.
    Two-lane roads
    Roads sized for wheeled carts, some widened to two lanes for traffic — four thousand years ago.
    Undeciphered script
    The Indus script remains untranslated, which is the single largest limit on what we can claim about this society. Everything about their religion and politics is inference.

    Decline — or transition?

    From about 1900 BCE the major cities changed. Archaeologists notice a decline in the maintenance of drains and roads at Harappa from that date, possibly because resources were spread too thin by decreased trade, overpopulation and climate change. Mohenjo-daro was abandoned around the same time — the river that supplied its fresh water changed course, which is reason enough to leave.

    The invasion theory, corrected. Scholars long theorized that Indo-Europeans invaded the region. We do have evidence of Indo-Europeans moving from the Eurasian steppes into the Indian subcontinent, bringing their languages, knowledge, horses and chariots. But modern genetic studies indicate they migrated and assimilated rather than invaded, and did not cause the population decline. (The article notes these genetic studies can be controversial and more data is needed.)

    The accepted view today: a combination of environmental factors — climate change, shifting tectonic plates, and rivers changing course. And importantly: the Indus communities did not die out or collapse. Populations migrated, trade declined, but the culture endured and transformed for many more years. Prefer the word transition over collapse when writing about the Indus Valley.

    How we know

    Archaeological, anthropological, artistic and genetic evidence — explicitly listed in the article as the four kinds. Excavated city layouts, drains, wells, the Great Bath, thousands of stamp seals, inscribed tablets, imported lapis and metals, and Harappan goods found abroad. What is missing is a readable text, which is why so many sentences about this society begin with "it's possible that."

    Five pairings that write themselves

    1. Geography — Mesopotamia vs. Egypt. Both river-valley farming societies, but unpredictable floods and no natural barriers made Mesopotamia's history a cycle of famine, rebellion and conquest, while the Nile's predictable flood and flanking deserts gave Egypt centuries of stable, centralized rule.

    2. Government — Mesopotamia vs. Nok. Mesopotamia built kings, armies and empires around the need to manage and defend surplus; Nok shows no kingship, palaces or temples at all, and may have handled disputes at shared ritual sites — complexity without a state.

    3. Religion — Shang China vs. Egypt. Both fused religion with rule, but the Shang king reached the divine through his ancestors and oracle bones, while the pharaoh was divine — and only Egypt made the afterlife a moral judgment.

    4. Trade — Aksum vs. Olmec. Aksum sat between two networks with a navy, coinage and a Red Sea port; the Olmec had no pack animals, no sailing ships and poor rivers, so everything moved on human backs — and only the most valuable goods moved at all.

    5. Government — Indus Valley vs. Egypt. Both coordinated enormous labor, but Egypt spent it on pyramids and images of the god-king while the Indus Valley spent it on drains, wells and grid streets — visible power versus invisible power.

    Vocabulary

    Every term from the Unit 1 study guide and the vocab slides, grouped so related words reinforce each other. Each definition ends with where you actually saw it, because the test rewards recognizing terms in context.

    People, food and the shift to farming

    Foraging
    Searching for food and supplies rather than growing them — hunting, fishing and gathering wild plants. Context: what everyone did before agriculture; East African communities kept doing it because resources were abundant.
    Foragers
    People who live by foraging. Largely egalitarian societies with relatively little inequality.
    Agriculture
    The practice of cultivating crops and raising animals for food.
    Agrarian
    Relating to agriculture, farmland and rural life. "Agrarian society" is the unit's label for every society studied here.
    Advent
    The arrival or beginning of something important. Context: "the advent of farming" — one of the most significant moments in human history.
    Domesticated
    Tamed and bred by humans for human use — animals for meat, milk, labor and transport; plants selected for better yields. Context: in Egypt, domestication of animals came before full farming societies.
    Pastoral
    Relating to herding livestock, often moving with the animals. Context: pastoralists north and west of Shang China were called "barbarians"; Nubians were partly pastoral; steppe pastoralists supplied horses to trade routes.
    Self-sufficient
    Able to supply your own needs without outside help. Context: the key contrast in the Growth of Cities article — villages are more self-sufficient; cities are highly interconnected. Rural Egyptians were more self-sufficient than city dwellers.
    Irrigation
    Supplying water to land through canals, ditches and channels so crops can grow where rainfall is insufficient. Context: the technology that let Mesopotamia farm a semi-arid plain — and that eventually salted its soil.
    Accumulate
    To gather or build up over time. Context: villagers began to accumulate private wealth and pass it to their children — the origin of social classes, proven by gold in children's graves.

    Places, land and environment

    Fertile Crescent
    The arc of fertile land through Southwest Asia, including Mesopotamia, where agriculture first developed. Some Egyptian crops may have originated there; farming knowledge spread from there toward East Africa.
    River delta
    The triangular area of fertile land where a river splits and empties into a sea. Context: the Nile Delta is Lower Egypt — note that "Lower" Egypt is in the north, because it is downriver.
    Infrastructure
    The built systems a society runs on — roads, bridges, canals, irrigation, drainage, walls, water supply. Context: Sargon's roads and postal system; the Indus Valley's wells and drains; the aqueducts and sewers cities were forced to invent.
    Decline
    A gradual loss of strength, population or importance. Context: used carefully for the Indus Valley, where the article prefers transition — the culture endured and transformed rather than dying.

    Materials, goods and technology

    Goods
    Physical items that are produced, bought, sold or traded.
    Obsidian
    Hard volcanic glass, flaked into extremely sharp tools, weapons, blades and even mirrors. Context: the specialty of Çatalhüyük; traded across Oceania; Mesoamerica's substitute for metal, and the source of Teotihuacan's power; also foraged in East Africa to trade with Egypt.
    Lapis lazuli
    A deep blue semi-precious stone quarried almost exclusively in modern Afghanistan. Context: the single best proof of long-distance trade in the unit — it appears in the Standard of Ur, in Nubian figurines, and at Harappa, thousands of miles from its only source.
    Ivory
    The hard white material of elephant tusks, carved into luxury goods. Context: East Africa's signature export; moved from inner Africa through Nubia to the Mediterranean, and through Aksum's port of Adulis.
    Bronze
    An alloy of copper and tin, harder than either — used for tools, weapons and ritual vessels. Context: the Shang Dynasty's signature technology, cast in dedicated foundries.
    Furnace
    An oven built to reach very high temperatures; an iron furnace heats ore to extract metal. Context: Bernard Fagg's unexpected discovery at Nok, with charcoal dated to 280 BCE (possibly 500 BCE), suggesting independent African invention of iron smelting.
    Slag
    The waste left over after smelting metal from ore. Context: metal slag found in Nok settlements is how archaeologists know iron was worked there.
    Pottery
    Objects made from fired clay. Enormously important to archaeologists because it survives when cloth and wood do not, and its style and distribution reveal trade and cultural connections. Context: a jump in Chavín pottery after 500 BCE signals rising food production.
    Terracotta
    Brownish-red baked clay, and the sculptures made from it. Context: the Nok figures — long heads, almond eyes, elaborate hairstyles, showing love, sickness, music and war.

    Writing, records and evidence

    Archaeology
    The study of the human past through excavation and analysis of physical remains.
    Anthropology
    The scientific study of human culture — beliefs, customs and the archaeological record.
    Artifact
    Any object made by human beings. The basic unit of archaeological evidence.
    Excavation
    A dig to uncover and recover ancient artifacts and structures. Context: excavation at Çatalhüyük and Tripolye revealed equal-sized homes; excavation in Egypt is hard because early sites lie below the water table.
    Cuneiform
    Mesopotamian wedge-shaped writing pressed into clay tablets, developed c. 3400 BCE. The world's first writing, invented for trade records.
    Tablet
    A flat slab of clay (or stone) written on and then dried or fired. Context: Mesopotamian tablets survived thousands of years because sun-baked clay is durable; Indus tablets broken in half may be business contracts.
    Hieroglyphics
    The ancient Egyptian pictorial script, reserved for religious purposes — as opposed to hieratic, used for everyday recordkeeping.
    Epic of Gilgamesh
    The first epic poem and oldest surviving work of literature (c. 2100 BCE), written in Akkadian: a king's hero's journey to discover the secret of eternal life. Its imagery of a figure strangling wild beasts also appears on Indus Valley seals.

    Religion and ritual

    Shaman
    A religious specialist believed to communicate with the spirit world, often through trance, ritual or divination. Context: Shang shamans read oracle bones; the Shang king eventually became the chief shaman; Chavín shaman-priests held the most authority and induced trances with mushrooms, coca leaves and cactus juice.
    Ziggurat
    A massive Mesopotamian stepped temple tower where priests and priestesses lived and performed rituals. The Ziggurat of Ur was dedicated to the moon god Nanna; Enheduanna was its high priestess.
    Ritual
    An established, prescribed ceremony, especially a religious one.
    Legitimate
    Accepted as rightful or lawful. Rulers needed to be seen as legitimate, which is why they claimed divine selection, divine descent, or divine identity. Context: "legitimacy" is the whole subject of the Authority & Control article — dynasty, religion and force are three ways of manufacturing it.

    Power and political organization

    Dynasty
    A line of rulers from the same family, passing the right to rule down through the family. Context: the Shang and Zhou in China; Egyptian dynasties of pharaohs; Han emperors tracing descent from Gaozu; Roman emperors adopted into dynasties to look legitimate.
    City-state
    A self-governing state made up of a city and its surrounding territory, operating independently in economic, political and cultural terms. Context: Eridu, Ur and Uruk in Sumer — independent, but sharing language and culture.
    Empire
    A single political unit under one supreme ruler, made up of different nations or peoples. Context: Sargon's Akkadian Empire was the world's first; Aksum, Rome, Persia and Han China are others.
    Theocracy
    Government whose rulers are chosen by, or claim to rule on behalf of, god(s).
    Nomadic
    Having no permanent home and moving from place to place. Context: nomadic people from the Zagros Mountains ended Sargon's dynasty after a famine weakened it.
    Tribute
    A required payment or "gift" of goods made to a governing authority. Context: smaller kingdoms paid tribute to Aksum's "king of kings."
    Hierarchy
    The ranking of people into social levels. Every society in this unit developed one once it produced surplus.
    Famine
    Extreme, widespread scarcity of food. Context: famine after Sargon's death weakened the Akkadian Empire; crop failure → famine → weakened kings → rebellion → invasion is the Mesopotamian cycle.
    Terms that get mixed up — check yourself

    Agrarian vs. pastoral: agrarian = growing crops on farmland; pastoral = herding animals, often on the move.
    City-state vs. empire: a city-state governs itself and its immediate territory; an empire rules different peoples under one supreme authority.
    Foraging vs. agriculture: searching for food vs. growing it.
    Obsidian vs. lapis lazuli: obsidian is black volcanic glass used for tools; lapis is a blue stone used for decoration and status.
    Bronze vs. slag: bronze is the useful alloy; slag is the waste.
    Hieroglyphics vs. hieratic vs. cuneiform: hieroglyphics = Egyptian religious script; hieratic = Egyptian everyday script; cuneiform = Mesopotamian wedge writing.
    Artifact vs. excavation: the artifact is the object; the excavation is the dig that recovers it.
    Upper vs. Lower Egypt: Upper Egypt is in the south (upriver), Lower Egypt in the north (the Delta). The Nile flows north.

    The transition to farming, villages and archaeology

    01What was life like in early farming villages, and how did it differ from hunter-gatherer communities?

    Farming villages were settled and permanent, with stored surplus food; hunter-gatherers were mobile with no storage. Villages started out relatively equal — similar house sizes, similar grave goods, similar diets from bone analysis — much like foraging societies.

    But village life was also closer quarters, so disease spread faster and life expectancy could fall, and people worried far more about weather because their food supply depended on it. Over time, permanent dwellings, ownership and inheritance produced private wealth and rigid kinship systems, and inequality grew.

    02What do we know about women's lives in early farming villages, and how did that change as villages grew?

    Early on, labor was divided by gender — women did more childcare while also producing food — but that division did not automatically mean inequality. Women were associated with fertility and the source of life, as shown by goddess figures such as the Seated Mother Goddess of Çatalhüyük.

    As villages grew, gender hierarchies intensified. Surplus food allowed more children, so women's work shifted from food production toward children and the household; male heads of family gained control of wealth; and strategic marriage alliances made family structures more rigid. Celebrating female fertility did not produce gender equality.

    03How do historians know that early villages were relatively equal — and how do they know when that stopped?

    Equality: at Çatalhüyük and in the Tripolye culture, homes were about the same size, objects in homes and graves were of roughly equal value, and bone analysis shows similar diets.

    Inequality: burial sites start to differ sharply. Some graves contain gold and jewelry while others contain nothing — and crucially, some of those rich graves belong to children. A child could not have earned wealth, so it must have been inherited, which proves wealth was being accumulated and passed down. That is the birth of social class.

    04Give examples of trade between villages, and explain how historians know it happened.

    Obsidian from Çatalhüyük made into tools and mirrors; obsidian, ceramics, shells and plants moved between islands by the Lapita culture (c. 1600–500 BCE) in Oceania; Norte Chico coastal villages in Peru trading fish, mollusks and shells inland for corn, textiles, potatoes, quinoa and llamas.

    Historians know because materials turn up far from their only geological source. Obsidian and lapis lazuli can be traced to specific origins, so finding them elsewhere is physical proof of exchange — no written record required.

    05Why is our picture of the ancient world biased, and what does that mean for societies like Nok and the Indus Valley?

    Because what survives determines what we know. Clothing and mud homes decay; pottery, stone, shell and metal last — so the record favors durable goods, which tend to belong to the wealthy. The record also favors societies that wrote things down and built in stone.

    Nok, Olmec, Chavín and the Indus Valley left no readable texts, so every claim about their religion and politics is an inference from objects. That does not make them less advanced — Nok may have invented iron smelting independently, and the Indus Valley had the best sanitation of the era. It makes them less documented, which is a fact about our evidence, not about them.

    Trade networks

    06Why was the Afro-Eurasian network larger than the Mesoamerican one? Give four reasons.

    (1) It contained the world's oldest agricultural societies, so its networks had far longer to develop. (2) Its geography could be crossed — wide plains, navigable rivers, passable mountains — while Mesoamerica had mountains and dense jungle. (3) It had domesticated pack animals: horses, cattle, elephants and Bactrian camels, versus none in Mesoamerica. (4) It had sailing technology on predictable seas — the Mediterranean and Indian Ocean — while Mesoamerica had canoes and coastal routes only.

    07How did goods move along these routes, and why was long-distance trade harder in Mesoamerica?

    In Afro-Eurasia, goods moved by pack animal (horses, cattle, elephants, camels) and by ship, usually as a relay in which each merchant worked one leg and handed the goods on. In Mesoamerica goods moved on human carriers' backs and in large canoes along coastal routes.

    Mesoamerica was harder because it had no domesticated pack animals, rivers unsuited to long-distance travel, no sailing ships, and mountains and dense jungle in the way. The Andes were the exception — the Chavín used llamas.

    08Name at least six goods traded across the Afro-Eurasian network and where each came from.

    Silk (China) · cotton and spices such as pepper and nutmeg (India) · frankincense, cinnamon and cloves (Arabia) · ivory and gold (East Africa) · grain (Egypt) · iron (Nubia and the upper Nile) · lapis lazuli (Central Asia / Bactria) · wine and glassware (Mediterranean) · horses (steppe pastoralists). Enslaved people were bought and sold in every region of the system.

    09How did long-distance trade change the communities it touched? Give two pieces of evidence.

    It produced wealth, cities, hierarchy and shared ideas, and it changed social hierarchies and community structure — creating merchant classes and new elite displays of status.

    Evidence 1: the Standard of Ur (c. 2500 BCE), made in Mesopotamia from lapis lazuli (Afghanistan), shells (Persian Gulf) and red marble (India) — a single object proving a network spanning thousands of miles. Evidence 2: Aksum, which went from a tiny settlement to one of the world's four great powers within a few centuries of the Red Sea becoming the main route, and whose gold coins have been found from Rome to Persia to India. A third option: Christianity reaching Aksum along trade routes — ideas traveled with goods.

    10How did rulers and empires benefit from trade, and what did they do about it?

    Trade brought the wealth and resources needed to maintain complex societies — cities and empires are expensive to run.

    So rulers invested in protecting it: they built roads and bridges, used armies to guard routes from bandits, and developed common weights, measures and coinage. Specific examples: Sargon standardized weights and measures and built roads; Aksum built a navy to patrol the Red Sea and minted its own coins.

    Cities and social class

    11Where did cities first appear, and where did they appear afterward?

    First in Mesopotamia and Egypt about 6,000 years ago; then China, India and Southeast Asia about 5,000 years ago; then Mesoamerica, the Andes and Sub-Saharan Africa 4,000–2,500 years ago. Cities were located for agriculture, trade routes and defensible sites.

    12Cities allowed job specialization, but city people still depended on rural farms. Why?

    Because cities did not grow their own food. Specialization is only possible if someone else produces a surplus — so the more specialized a city became, the more dependent it was on the countryside, not less.

    The article's example: a shoemaker depends on farmers for food, soldiers for safety and merchants for materials. This is also why conquest expanded agricultural production — more land, resources and labor to feed the city. And it changed where people lived: in villages you live with family; in cities you live near your job.

    13Explain the "urban archipelago" metaphor — and explain where it breaks down.

    An archipelago is a group of islands. Cities were islands of dense population and wealth in a sea of countryside, connected to each other by trade routes rather than by the land between them — a city often had more contact with a distant city than with nearby farms.

    Where it fails: islands are genuinely separate, but cities were completely dependent on their surrounding countryside for food, labor and raw materials. The land between cities was not empty water, and writers who described city and countryside as opposites were wrong — the two were always deeply interconnected.

    14Why did complex societies develop social classes when those categories had never existed before? (Essential question 4)

    Because surplus food made specialization possible, and specialization made people unequal. Once not everyone had to farm, some people became scribes, soldiers, priests, artisans and merchants — roles with different value, different access to wealth and different closeness to power.

    Three forces locked it in: (1) accumulation — surplus can be stored, owned and inherited, which is why gold appears in children's graves; (2) coordination — irrigation, walls and long-distance trade required administrators and leaders, who gained authority; (3) justification — priests explained why the ruler belonged on top and soldiers enforced it. Every society in the unit that produced surplus produced hierarchy, which is strong evidence the cause is structural rather than cultural.

    Government, laws and control

    15Why did Ashoka build and inscribe his pillars, when most people could not read them?

    The inscriptions proclaimed peace and Buddhist guidance, but the monuments themselves symbolized his absolute power. Even an illiterate subject could see an enormous stone pillar erected by imperial order and understand who was in charge.

    The general principle: empires use visible displays of power to maintain control. Compare Aksum's stelae and Egypt's temple carvings — monuments are arguments about authority.

    16How did ancient emperors use family as a method of control?

    Through dynasties — family lines of rulers — usually passing power through the male line. People had to accept the family's right to rule, so descent became the argument for legitimacy.

    Han emperors traced descent from the first Han ruler, Gaozu. Roman emperors, who were often chosen by other means, were adopted into dynasties specifically so they would appear legitimate — which shows how important the appearance of bloodline was, even when the bloodline was fictional.

    17What challenges did women face when they ruled empires?

    Legitimacy was built on male succession, so empresses were often confined to producing heirs. Women who did rule had to manufacture legitimacy that male rulers simply inherited — and faced resistance during and after their reigns.

    Examples: Empress Theodora (Eastern Rome) as an influential adviser; Empress Lü (Han China) ruling as regent after Gaozu's death; Hatshepsut (Egypt) taking the full title of king and ruling as a male-presenting pharaoh, with carvings showing the gods endorsing her coronation. The resistance is documented: later rulers tried to erase her inscriptions, and ancient graffiti at her tomb shows disapproval of female rule.

    18How did emperors use religion to increase their authority? Give three different mechanisms.

    (1) Divine right — claiming the gods chose them. The Mandate of Heaven in Zhou China; Cyrus the Great claiming Babylon's own god Marduk chose him, which legitimized him to the conquered.

    (2) Dominating or enforcing worship — the Hittites seized idols from conquered lands; the Neo-Assyrians forced worship of Assur; Rome required public sacrifices as a loyalty test, punishing refusal, which fell hardest on Christians.

    (3) Unification — Ashoka spread Buddhism to bind a diverse empire together, and Aksum adopted Christianity as a state religion, aligning itself with Rome.

    19Why did empires care about controlling the periphery, and what two methods did they use?

    To collect taxes, prevent revolts and recruit soldiers.

    Method 1 — local rulers: early Rome let conquered kings keep their thrones; Aksum's "king of kings" left smaller kings in place but took tribute, stationed warriors in each region, and met the regional kings in person to receive pledges of support. Method 2 — direct control: later Rome replaced local kings with Roman officials.

    When it failed, the response was force: the Jewish Revolt (66–73 CE), caused by heavy taxes and a failure to protect the population, ended with Jerusalem destroyed, roughly a million deaths, and Masada fallen.

    20The article names three methods empires used to increase control. What are they, and what other methods can you think of?

    The three: dynastic families (real or apparent bloodline), religion (divine legitimacy, enforced worship, unifying belief), and force or violence (armies, punishment, destruction).

    Other defensible answers, all supported by the unit: infrastructure (Sargon's roads and postal system tie territory to the center); standardization (one official language, one set of weights and measures, one coinage); economic dependence (controlling trade routes so the periphery needs the center); bureaucracy (Shang officials, Aksumite administrators collecting tribute); monumental display (Ashoka's pillars, Aksumite stelae); hostage-taking and strategic marriage; and selective toleration — letting people keep their customs so long as they stay loyal, as the Persians did.

    The societies

    21How did the geography of Mesopotamia encourage early societies to develop — and how did it also destroy them?

    Encouraged: the Tigris and Euphrates flooded annually with snowmelt from the Zagros Mountains, leaving fertile silt. Flat land plus irrigation and canals allowed large-scale farming of wheat and barley, producing surpluses that could feed cities and be traded for the metal, wood and stone the region lacked.

    Destroyed: flooding was unpredictable and destructive; the semi-arid climate plus irrigation caused salt to build up in the soil, ruining harvests; and there were almost no natural barriers. So crop failure led to famine, famine weakened kings and sparked rebellion, and invaders took advantage of every moment of weakness. Same geography, both outcomes.

    22Describe the political structure of Mesopotamia and explain how the environment shaped it.

    Independent city-states — Eridu, Ur, Uruk — each ruled by a king, later unified into the Akkadian Empire under Sargon (c. 2334–2279 BCE).

    The environment created that structure: surplus crops had to be stored, distributed and defended; someone had to direct irrigation systems, canals and walls; city-states fought each other over access to water and land, so each needed a king commanding soldiers; and because the harvest depended on rivers nobody could predict, kings claimed to speak with the gods to secure divine protection for agriculture. Government here is essentially water management with an army attached.

    23List Sargon's innovations and explain why each strengthened his empire.

    (1) The first standing army (~5,000 soldiers) — permanent force available to conquer and to crush rebellions. (2) The first postal system — communication with distant officials, so the center could actually govern the periphery. (3) An official language (Akkadian) — administrative uniformity. (4) Roads — movement of troops, messages and goods. (5) Standardized weights and measures — fair, predictable commerce, which made trade flourish and generated wealth. He also placed trusted people in power, including his daughter Enheduanna as high priestess at Ur, tying religious authority to his family.

    The limits: he needed the army for constant rebellions, and after his death a famine weakened the empire until nomads from the Zagros defeated his dynasty around 2150 BCE.

    24Why is the Shang considered China's first dynasty despite texts mentioning the Xia?

    Because there is no archaeological evidence for the Xia (c. 2070–1600 BCE) — only later textual mentions. For the Shang (1600–1046 BCE) there is physical evidence: walled cities, tombs, thousands of bronze artifacts, and oracle bones, corroborated by Chinese histories written in the 5th–1st centuries BCE.

    The underlying lesson is about method: historians distinguish between what texts claim and what evidence confirms.

    25What role did oracle bones play in Shang society, and what do they tell us?

    Role: divination. A shaman scratched a question onto an ox scapula or turtle shell, heated it until it cracked, and read the cracks as an answer from the gods or ancestors. The question, the asker and the answer were then engraved on the bone.

    What they tell us: three things at once. (1) Religion — animism, ancestor worship, and the king's role as spiritual leader, since he eventually became the chief diviner. (2) Writing — they carry the earliest Chinese script, pictograms closely resembling modern Chinese characters. (3) Daily concerns of the elite — harvests, military strategy, taxation, trade, even the gender of a royal child — plus astronomical records of eclipses and solstices used to build a 365-day solar calendar for farming.

    26What does Lady Fu Hao's tomb reveal about the status and role of women in Shang society?

    That exceptional women could hold real military and religious power — but that it was exceptional, not typical. Fu Hao, a consort of King Wu Ding (c. 1250 BCE), led her own troops into battle and performed sacrifices, and is named on close to 200 oracle bones.

    Her tomb at Yinxu, found intact in 1976, held 16 servants and 6 dogs as sacrificial burials, ~500 bronze objects including 130 weapons, over 700 jade items, ivory, 500+ bone ornaments and 6,900 cowry shells. Note how the evidence converges: the weapons corroborate the oracle bones' claim that she was a general, the cowry shells prove long-distance trade, and the sacrificial burials show beliefs about the afterlife and the status of enslaved people. The article is careful to say her power came from her own skills and her relationship with the king.

    27How did the Zhou justify overthrowing the Shang, and why is that idea so significant?

    King Wu of the Zhou claimed the last Shang king had lost the Mandate of Heaven (tian ming) — that he was corrupt and focused on his own pleasure at the expense of his people, and so had lost the virtue that gave him the right to rule. The Zhou won at the Battle of Muye in 1046 BCE and ruled for almost 800 years.

    It matters because it makes divine right conditional and revocable. Most claims of divine legitimacy protect the ruler permanently; the Mandate of Heaven contains a built-in justification for rebellion — if a dynasty falls, that itself proves heaven withdrew its approval.

    28Give three reasons people settled along the Nile, and explain what a cataract is and why it mattered.

    Three reasons: fresh water for people and animals; fertile soil from the annual flood, plus water for irrigation; and a transport route for trade. Add a fourth if you like: climate change dried the surrounding region, so the Nile was where the resources were.

    A cataract is a stretch of shallow, rocky rapids in the Nile. Cataracts blocked boat travel, so they acted as natural borders, defensive barriers and chokepoints controlling trade. Nubia's A-Group culture is defined by its location between the first and second cataracts.

    29What was Nubia, and how was it related to Egypt?

    Nubia was a region south of Upper Egypt in modern Sudan, between the first and second cataracts. Its A-Group culture (c. 3800–2900 BCE) was one of the earliest farming societies in the Indo-Mediterranean world, known from about 200 sites, mostly cemeteries.

    The two were closely connected and mutually influential, not ruler and subject: similar artifacts, written symbols, artistic features and ideas about rulers; shared burial customs (bodies facing west, offerings for the afterlife); shared reverence for the goddess Isis; a shared necropolis at Abydos; possible common origins for their scripts; and established trade networks, with Nubia moving ivory from inner Africa toward the Mediterranean. In both societies women were unusually well regarded.

    30Why is the study of ancient Egypt difficult, and what helps archaeologists?

    Difficult because: Egyptian history is extremely long, so "ancient Egypt" spans thousands of years and very different periods; many early sites now lie below the current water level, making excavation hard; and the Predynastic period left no writing.

    What helps: a dry climate that preserves organic material; the practice of burying goods with the dead, creating sealed collections of everyday objects; readable scripts (hieroglyphic and hieratic); and monumental architecture and art that survived above ground. The Narmer Palette is the classic case — one carved stone that caused historians to revise the timeline of unification.

    31Explain the Egyptian concept of duality and its consequences for women.

    Duality is the idea that reality is made of balanced, complementary opposites: Upper and Lower Egypt, life and afterlife, male and female. Egyptians applied it to land, rulers and daily life.

    Because male and female were treated as complementary rather than ranked, Egyptian women reached a status unknown in most ancient societies: they owned land and businesses, held important religious and agricultural roles, and received equal pay for equal work — something most industrialized countries still have not achieved. Nubian women were similarly well regarded, honored as givers of life, experts in agriculture, and leaders of fertility and rebirth rituals.

    32Take the Egyptian weighing of the heart ceremony and identify every religious pattern it demonstrates.

    The ceremony: after death, in the Hall of Truth, the heart is weighed against the feather of Ma'at before gods including Osiris. Lighter than the feather → the Field of Reeds. Heavier → the heart is eaten by Ammut and the person passes into nothingness.

    MG (moral guidance) — it establishes a moral code with consequences, reinforced by the 42 Negative Confessions in which the dead affirm their integrity. L&AL (daily life and afterlife) — it shapes burial practice, mummification and how people lived. R&S (ritual and symbolism) — a scale, a feather and a monster embody abstract ideas of justice and eternity. You could also argue R2D indirectly, since the gods conducting judgment are the same gods who legitimize the pharaoh.

    33What do the Olmec colossal heads tell us about Olmec society, beyond the fact that they liked sculpture?

    They are ~20-ton basalt portraits, quarried in the Sierra de los Tuxtlas Mountains and moved 60–70 km to Olmec sites — with no pack animals and no wheeled transport.

    That requires the ability to command an enormous coordinated workforce, an agricultural surplus to feed it, and the political authority to direct it — so the heads are evidence of hierarchy, economy and government at once. Their distinctive individual features also suggest they depict specific rulers rather than gods, since Olmec gods were shown as blended human-animal figures.

    34How do we know Chavín de Huántar was a ritual site rather than a city?

    By what is missing. Archaeologists found no hearths, no trash, no dwellings — none of the ordinary domestic debris people leave behind. The dwellings and agricultural sites are outside the complex.

    What they did find fits ritual use: staircases, archways and maze-like underground corridors, platforms where a shaman conducted ceremonies before processions of visitors, stone slabs used for bloodletting, and imagery of real and mythical animals — jaguars, snakes, eagles, a winged crocodile. Renovations after 500 BCE suggest a growing number of participants. This is a good example of absence of evidence used as evidence.

    35How and why did agrarian society form late in the Aksum region — and what changed everything?

    Late because the region was rich. Abundant resources meant communities could keep foraging and herding; some were reluctant to farm because it meant a less healthy, more work-filled life. They domesticated ensete by ~3000 BCE and foraged hides, feathers, myrrh and obsidian to trade with Egypt; by 2000 BCE most were semi-nomadic, mixing foraging, farming and pastoralism. Farming knowledge eventually arrived from Egypt and Southwest Asia along trade networks — collective learning.

    What changed everything: 30 BCE, when Rome conquered Egypt and trade shifted toward the Red Sea. Aksum became the hub between the Roman Mediterranean and India, grew wealthy enough to build a navy, mint coins, and expand into an empire — one of the world's four great powers by the 3rd century CE.

    36What does the evidence from Aksum tell us about how it formed and maintained communities and networks?

    Networks: Aksumite gold coins found from Rome to Persia to India prove the reach of its trade; the port of Adulis exchanged African interior goods (ivory, gold, perfume, exotic animals) for wine, olive oil, foreign coins and bronze lamps; a navy protected the routes.

    Community and state: unification under kings, with a "king of kings" receiving tribute from smaller kingdoms, warriors stationed in each region to prevent uprisings, and in-person meetings where regional kings pledged support. Stelae up to 23 m marking elite tombs show a clear social hierarchy — bigger monument, higher status — and imply roles for bureaucrats, priests, soldiers, merchants and artisans. Religiously: animism gave way to astronomical gods and then to Christianity as state religion in the 4th century CE, arriving along trade routes and blending with local customs.

    37Why do we know so little about complex societies in West Africa before the 10th century CE, and what does Nok prove?

    Why little is known: later invasions and conflict destroyed records; history in the region was carried by oral tradition rather than writing; and much of what we do know about Ghana and Mali comes from North African Muslim writers who crossed the Sahara by the 10th century — so societies before outside observers arrived went unrecorded. Since farming began in West Africa perhaps 4,000 years ago, a 3,000-year gap before the first complex society is implausible.

    What Nok proves: that a society can be technologically and artistically sophisticated — independently developed iron smelting (furnace charcoal dated to 280 BCE, possibly 500 BCE), a refined and consistent terracotta tradition, a dense population — with no cities, no kingship, no palaces, no temples and no writing. It forces you to separate "complex" from "state."

    38How was the Indus Valley governed, and what is the evidence?

    We do not know for certain, and the reason matters: the Indus script is undeciphered. But the physical evidence points somewhere unusual.

    Neither Harappa nor Mohenjo-daro had a central palace or temple, so the leading interpretation is that the society was run by a number of wealthy families rather than a single ruler — consistent with the thousands of stamp seals that appear to identify prominent families and mark their goods. Yet the cities show enormous coordination: grid streets, city-wide wells, an advanced drainage system carrying sewage outside the city, roads sized for wheeled carts, and sections raised on flood mounds. So authority clearly existed — it just invested in infrastructure rather than in monuments to itself.

    39Why did the Indus Valley society decline — and why is "decline" the wrong word?

    The accepted explanation is a combination of environmental factors: climate change, shifting tectonic plates, and rivers changing course — the river supplying Mohenjo-daro moved, which is reason enough to abandon a city. At Harappa, maintenance of drains and roads declines from about 1900 BCE, likely because resources were spread thin by decreased trade, overpopulation and climate change.

    The old Indo-European invasion theory has been corrected: genetic studies indicate Indo-Europeans migrated and assimilated, bringing languages, horses and chariots, rather than invading — and did not cause the population decline. (The article notes these studies are debated and more data is needed.)

    "Decline" is misleading because the communities did not die out or collapse. Populations migrated and trade fell, but the culture endured and transformed for many more years. Prefer transition.

    40Compare the role of geography in Mesopotamia and Egypt. (Model 10-point answer)

    Claim: Mesopotamia and Egypt were both river-valley farming societies whose surpluses produced cities and kings, but the predictability of the river and the presence of natural barriers made Egypt stable and centralized while Mesopotamia's history became a repeating cycle of famine, rebellion and conquest.

    Mesopotamia. The Tigris and Euphrates flooded with Zagros snowmelt, leaving fertile silt, and irrigation extended farmland across a flat semi-arid plain, producing wheat and barley surpluses. But the flooding was unpredictable and destructive, and irrigating dry flat land caused salt to build up in the soil until harvests failed. The region also had almost no natural barriers — no protective mountains or seas. Crop failure meant famine; famine weakened the king's authority and sparked rebellion; and invaders seized those moments, which is why the region passed from city-states to Akkadians, Babylonians, Assyrians, Persians and finally Alexander the Great in 331 BCE.

    Egypt. The Nile, the longest river in the world, flooded predictably, depositing silt that supported dense farming along a narrow corridor — celebrated in the Hymn to the Nile and in the god Hapi, "Lord of the River Bringing Vegetation." Egypt was flanked by desert on both sides and its southern approach was interrupted by cataracts, rocky rapids that acted as borders and chokepoints. Protected and reliably fed, Egypt unified under Narmer around 3200–3000 BCE — recorded on the Narmer Palette — and sustained centralized dynastic rule for millennia.

    Explanation. The difference is not the presence of a river but its behavior and defensibility. A predictable flood lets a state plan, tax and build; an unpredictable one makes every harvest a gamble on the ruler's competence. Barriers let a state spend surplus on monuments and administration; their absence forces it to spend on armies, and still lose. This shows up in religion too: Mesopotamian kings had to keep claiming to speak with the gods to secure the harvest, while pharaohs could simply be divine.

    Big idea. Both confirm the unit's chain — surplus creates specialists, specialists create hierarchy, hierarchy requires justification — but they show that geography sets how durable the resulting structure is.

    41Contrast government in Mesopotamia and Nok. (Model 10-point answer)

    Claim: Mesopotamia and Nok were both agrarian societies with specialized production, but Mesopotamia developed highly centralized, visible political authority while Nok shows no evidence of centralized rule at all — a difference driven partly by what each environment demanded and partly by what evidence survives.

    Mesopotamia. Surplus grain had to be stored, distributed and defended, and irrigation systems, canals and walls had to be directed by someone — so each city-state (Eridu, Ur, Uruk) had a king who ran large projects, commanded soldiers against rival city-states competing for water and land, and claimed to speak with the gods. Under Sargon of Akkad (c. 2334–2279 BCE) this scaled into the world's first empire, held together by a 5,000-soldier standing army, a postal system, an official language, roads and standardized weights and measures. Authority was displayed in ziggurats and in a rigid social pyramid from kings and priests down to enslaved people.

    Nok. Nok society was not built around cities but around dispersed settlements centered on extended families, each with its own farm and cattle. There is no evidence of kingship, palaces, or even temples signifying a ruling class. What archaeologists find instead are ritual sites far from settlements, marked by one or several deliberately buried sculptures, which people may have travelled to in order to make agreements or hear disputes. There may have been status differences — expert blacksmiths, possibly a terracotta specialist guild — but experts disagree, and no ruling class appears.

    Explanation. Two causes work together. First, necessity: Mesopotamia's survival depended on large-scale irrigation and on defending wealth in an indefensible landscape, and coordination on that scale creates rulers. Nok's family-farm settlements needed no comparable coordination, so authority could stay local. Second, evidence: Mesopotamia wrote on clay that lasted 5,000 years, while Nok's history was carried by oral tradition, so a Nok political structure could exist and leave no trace. Honest answers name both.

    Big idea. Nok complicates essential question 4. Surplus and specialization normally produce hierarchy — but Nok shows a society could be technologically sophisticated, independently smelting iron by 280–500 BCE, without the visible state that Mesopotamia treated as inevitable.

    42Compare religion in Shang China and Egypt. (Outline the answer yourself, then check.)

    Similarity to lead with: in both, religion and political authority were the same system — the ruler's power came from his relationship to the divine (pattern R2D), and both societies buried goods with the dead because they believed in an afterlife (L&AL).

    Shang evidence: animism plus ancestor worship — the dead joined the spirit world and were asked for guidance. Oracle bones: a shaman scratched a question on an ox scapula or turtle shell, heated it, and read the cracks. The king was both political and spiritual leader and eventually became the chief shaman. Tombs held tools, weapons, instruments and ornaments; Fu Hao's held 16 servants and 6 dogs as sacrifices.

    Egyptian evidence: polytheism explaining nature — Hapi for the flood, Ra battling Apophis nightly. The pharaoh was a god-king, drawn the same size as the gods in the crowning of Ramses III. An elaborate afterlife: mummification, canopic jars guarded by the Four Sons of Horus, the Book of the Dead, and the weighing of the heart against the feather of Ma'at.

    The two differences that earn points. (1) Route to the divine: the Shang king reached the gods through his ancestors, which made the family line itself sacred and dynastic succession religiously natural; the pharaoh did not petition the divine — he was divine. (2) Morality: Egyptian religion made the afterlife a moral judgment — the 42 Negative Confessions and the weighing of the heart tested how you had lived. Shang practice centered on divination and correct ritual: the question was what to do next, not whether you had been good.

    Optional third point: both used religion to justify power, but only China developed the Mandate of Heaven, which made divine approval revocable and turned rebellion into a legitimate correction.
